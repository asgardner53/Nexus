# Chapter 11 Wombat-9000 Review

## Organisations Where Knowledge Can Move

Status: targeted developmental review
Verdict: concept survives, but Selective Permeability must be defended more explicitly against the 'knowledge management with a wombat' objection

## Overall judgement

Chapter 11 survives the sceptical-reader test, but this is the first chapter where the central author synthesis is close enough to established management constructs that the distinction must be made explicitly rather than assumed.

The chapter is not merely knowledge management with different language.

Knowledge management is broader and typically concerns the creation, capture, storage, transfer, sharing and use of organisational knowledge.

Chapter 11's narrower problem is different:

> How can an organisation preserve specialist boundaries because they create depth and accountability, while making those boundaries selectively permeable when consequential problems require knowledge to cross them?

That tension — preservation plus selective crossing — is the chapter's distinctive contribution.

The chapter should be revised, not rewritten.

## The Wombat test

Question:

> What does Selective Permeability explain that absorptive capacity, knowledge transfer, boundary spanning and knowledge integration do not already explain?

Answer:

Selective Permeability is not a replacement for any of those constructs.

- Absorptive capacity explains how organisations recognise, assimilate/transform and exploit external knowledge.
- Knowledge transfer research explains factors that affect movement of knowledge between sources and recipients.
- Boundary spanning explains activities that connect units, teams or organisations across boundaries.
- Knowledge integration explains how differentiated knowledge is combined for coordinated action or innovation.

Selective Permeability adds a book-level design principle tying those mechanisms to the central tension of The Polymath Advantage:

> boundaries create specialist depth and accountability, yet complex problems often require some knowledge to cross those same boundaries.

Its explanatory job is therefore not 'how knowledge moves' in general. It is 'how much permeability is needed, where, for what problem, while preserving the value of the boundary itself'.

That is sufficiently distinctive to keep — but only if the manuscript says this explicitly.

## Wombat bite 1: define the difference from knowledge management in the manuscript

The chapter currently says it is not a generic knowledge-management text, but the distinction is mostly implicit.

Recommendation:

Add one short paragraph near the introduction of Selective Permeability:

> Knowledge management asks a broader question about how organisations create, capture, share and use knowledge. The narrower concern here is what happens when the boundary that creates specialist depth is also the boundary a consequential problem needs to cross. Selective permeability is the book's name for managing that tension, not a replacement for established knowledge-management concepts.

This one paragraph would neutralise the strongest sceptical objection.

## Wombat bite 2: keep Selective Permeability lightly branded

The term earns its place as a synthesis, but repeating it too often will make it sound like a consultancy product.

Recommendation:

Define it once, use it at the conceptual centre and again in the conclusion. Elsewhere use ordinary language such as 'relevant knowledge movement', 'permeable boundary' or 'cross-boundary pathway'.

## Wombat bite 3: the silo definition is useful but currently too definitional

Current line:

> A silo is not a boundary. It is a boundary that has stopped useful knowledge moving.

Strong, memorable and useful — but it sounds like a universal definition of a contested organisational term.

Recommendation:

Shift to:

> For the purposes of this argument, the useful distinction is between a boundary that preserves depth and one that prevents relevant knowledge from moving.

Then preserve the punchier line as author shorthand, perhaps:

> In that sense, a silo is a boundary that has stopped useful knowledge moving.

This avoids pretending the book has settled the literature's definition of silo.

## Wombat bite 4: absorptive capacity is doing legitimate work

The Stettler et al. section is strong and appropriately bounded.

It does something Chapter 9 cannot: it moves from individual access/understanding to organisational ability to recognise and use external knowledge.

Protect:

> An organisation can drown in access while starving for understanding.

The inference from stronger absorptive-capacity effects in knowledge-rich environments is clearly labelled synthesis.

No new research required.

## Wombat bite 5: 'the bottleneck moves' is strong but should remain interpretive

The section implies that as information inflow gets cheaper, selection and integration become more important.

That is a compelling synthesis, but not a direct result of Stettler et al.

Recommendation:

Keep it in narrative voice and avoid immediately attaching the citation to the aphoristic claim.

Current structure is mostly safe.

## Wombat bite 6: the hidden knowledge system section needs an evidence anchor or less research-like wording

The claims about formal and informal networks are plausible and well established in organisational research, but the current chapter does not cite a network-transfer source.

Options:

A. verify and add Reagans & McEvily (2003) or another clean primary source; or
B. keep the section as illustrative organisational synthesis and remove language that sounds like an empirical literature summary.

Recommendation:

Prefer A if source verification is easy. This is the one evidence gap worth filling.

## Wombat bite 7: decision authority is the best new contribution — but distinguish evidence from synthesis

The entire passage beginning with the boundary between knowing and deciding is excellent.

It materially strengthens the chapter because it shows why knowledge sharing can become decorative.

Protect:

> Relevant knowledge needs a path not only into conversation but into authority.

And:

> Knowledge must be able to cross toward the decision. The decision must remain exposed to knowledge capable of constraining it.

These are author synthesis.

The chapter does not currently cite direct evidence for this specific decision-rights proposition, and that is acceptable if the text remains clearly argumentative rather than empirical.

Do not invent a citation simply to make it look scientific.

## Wombat bite 8: 'specialist evidence can be overruled, by whom and on what basis' risks drifting into governance doctrine

The point is sound, but wording can sound prescriptive across legal, safety and professional contexts.

Recommendation:

Use:

> The organisation should be clear about how specialist evidence enters a decision, what authority it carries and how disagreement or override is handled.

This is more general and avoids implying one governance model.

## Wombat bite 9: ambidexterity evidence is legitimate but the chapter can trim the detail

Junni, Fourné and Wenke provide exactly the needed guardrail against 'one structure fits all'.

The chapter does not need three studies at equal narrative length.

Recommendation:

Keep all three citations if desired, but compress approximately 100-140 words. The point is contingency, not a literature review of ambidexterity.

Protect:

> An organisation needs places where knowledge can wander and places where work cannot.

This is clearly author synthesis.

## Wombat bite 10: incentive claims are broader than the one cited meta-analysis

Shen et al. supports antecedents and consequences of knowledge hiding, but the manuscript gives several specific examples about promotion, local performance and cross-boundary contribution that are illustrative rather than directly evidenced by that meta-analysis.

Recommendation:

Make the transition explicit:

> The meta-analysis establishes that organisational and leadership conditions matter. The particular incentive tensions below are practical illustrations of how misalignment can appear.

This keeps evidence and inference clean.

## Wombat bite 11: connective capacity is useful; avoid creating another new construct

> The organisation needs connective capacity, not a single connective genius.

Excellent line.

But do not capitalise or formally define 'Connective Capacity'. It should remain ordinary language supporting Selective Permeability, not become a second branded concept.

## Wombat bite 12: the broad-person dependency example is excellent

The passage about organisations depending on a few unusually broad people is one of the chapter's most original applications of the book's thesis.

Protect:

> For a while, the system works because they work. Then one of them leaves.

This makes the difference between individual polymathic talent and organisational capability concrete.

## Wombat bite 13: AI section is correctly restrained

PASS.

The manuscript explicitly says Stettler et al. did not study AI and uses the evidence as a warning about knowledge-rich environments rather than an AI-specific finding.

No changes beyond possible tightening.

## Wombat bite 14: organisational polymathic capability is distinct enough if the manuscript states its ancestry

The final definition survives:

> Organisational polymathic capability is the capacity of an organisation to preserve differentiated depth while moving relevant knowledge across boundaries so that complex problems can be reframed, integrated and acted on responsibly.

But immediately after definition, add a sentence such as:

> This does not replace absorptive capacity, knowledge transfer, boundary spanning or knowledge integration; it describes how those mechanisms contribute to the narrower problem developed in this book: preserving depth while enabling consequential cross-boundary reasoning.

That is the sentence that earns the construct.

## Wombat bite 15: 'organisational extension of epistemic mobility' is rhetorically attractive but conceptually too strong

Epistemic mobility was defined at the person level as changing lenses/representations.

An organisation is not literally a person changing lenses.

Recommendation:

Change:

> That is the organisational extension of epistemic mobility.

To:

> It is the organisational condition that allows epistemic mobility and collective polymathy to matter beyond individual people and teams.

This preserves conceptual boundaries.

## Wombat bite 16: the ending is excellent

Protect the final movement:

> The polymathic organisation is not one in which knowledge moves freely. It is one in which knowledge can move intelligently.

And the final return to specialisation:

> Preserve the depth created by division while rebuilding enough connection for complex problems to become whole again.

These lines bring the book's argument full circle.

Decision-authority section: PASS AS AUTHOR SYNTHESIS. Do not fake empirical backing.

## 'Knowledge management with a wombat?' verdict

NO — provided the targeted distinction is added.

Why not?

Knowledge management is the broad parent territory.

Chapter 11's specific theoretical contribution is the tension between:

1. boundaries as necessary infrastructure for depth, professional authority, repeatability and accountability; and
2. boundaries as possible barriers when complex problems require relevant knowledge to cross them.

Selective Permeability names the design principle that follows:

> make the boundary permeable where the problem requires knowledge transfer, while keeping it intact where depth, confidentiality, execution or accountability require separation.

That is narrower than knowledge management and tightly integrated with the book's preceding theory.

The term earns its place if presented as synthesis rather than novelty-by-rebranding.

## Originality verdict

PASS WITH EXPLICIT ANCESTRY REQUIRED.

The chapter should say openly that its mechanism draws on established literatures including absorptive capacity, knowledge transfer, boundary spanning/integration, knowledge hiding and ambidexterity.

The originality lies in assembling them around the book's depth-versus-connection problem, not claiming the underlying organisational mechanisms are new.
