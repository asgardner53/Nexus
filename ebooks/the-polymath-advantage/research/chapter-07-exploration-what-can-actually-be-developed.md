# Chapter 7 Exploration

## What Can Actually Be Developed?

Status: exploratory research note
Purpose: test the book's founding proposition that polymathic capability is developable without overstating trainability or denying dispositional, cognitive and environmental influences.

## Emerging chapter problem

The first six chapters have decomposed polymathic capability into several mechanisms: depth, breadth, epistemic mobility, structural transfer and calibration.

Chapter 7 must now answer the question the book has been carrying from the beginning:

> If polymathic capability is not an elite identity, what parts of it can a person genuinely develop?

A weak chapter would answer yes and turn immediately to habits.

A stronger chapter must resist the binary.

The evidence suggests that relevant traits, skills and practices differ in malleability. Some can improve through learning or structured intervention. Some are partly influenced by relatively stable individual differences. Some improvements remain narrow and do not automatically transfer into wider capability.

The chapter should therefore treat developability as a question of components, not as a claim that 'polymathy' itself has been experimentally trained.

## Emerging governing thesis

> **Polymathic capability is developable in parts, not trainable as a single proven trait.**

Development changes the range of what a person can do and the likelihood that useful cross-domain thinking will occur. It does not erase individual differences or guarantee identical outcomes.

A second formulation worth exploring:

> **Development changes the odds. It does not make people interchangeable.**

This could become an important safeguard against both biological determinism and unlimited-potential rhetoric.

## 1. Personality is relatively stable and still capable of change

Openness to experience is relevant because it includes curiosity, imagination and receptivity to novelty, all intuitively adjacent to polymathic behaviour.

The evidence does not support treating openness as either fixed or freely trainable.

Jackson and Wright's 2024 review describes personality as relatively stable across the lifespan while also finding ample evidence of malleability. They distinguish mechanisms that can support change from forces that maintain stability (Jackson & Wright, 2024).

A 2024 systematic review of volitional personality change similarly concludes that self-directed trait change can occur, including through relatively modest interventions, but effects vary by trait and the evidence base remains young. In that review, estimated effects for openness were smaller and less precise than for some other traits, with wide confidence intervals and few available effect sizes (Haehner, Wright & Bleidorn, 2024).

This is useful because it gives the chapter a nuanced position:

- openness may matter;
- openness is not destiny;
- evidence that openness itself can be deliberately shifted is not strong enough to carry the whole developability argument.

Potential line:

> A disposition can influence where someone starts without determining where they must finish.

## 2. Genetic influence is not genetic destiny

Large-scale genetic research confirms that personality traits, including openness, have detectable genetic components.

A recent large-scale GWAS across hundreds of thousands of participants identified widespread genetic correlates of the Big Five. Common genetic variants accounted for a minority of variance in each trait, with estimates varying by trait and method (Revived Genomics of Personality Consortium, 2025).

Older twin and genomic studies have reported higher heritability estimates under different designs, which is a reminder that heritability is population- and method-dependent rather than a fixed percentage attached to an individual.

This section must be handled carefully.

The chapter should explicitly explain:

- heritability describes variation in a population under particular conditions;
- it does not mean a trait is immutable;
- genetic influence and environmental influence coexist;
- gene-environment transactions can reinforce or alter developmental pathways.

Do not turn Chapter 7 into behavioural genetics.

The point is narrower:

> Biological and dispositional differences are part of the starting conditions, not a verdict on developmental possibility.

## 3. Practice matters, but practice is not everything

The deliberate-practice literature gives us a powerful anti-hype anchor.

Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald's meta-analysis found that deliberate practice explained meaningful but highly variable proportions of performance differences across domains: larger shares in games, music and sports, smaller shares in education, and less than 1% of variance in the professional-performance studies included in their analysis (Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald, 2014).

The exact percentages should not be stretched into a universal decomposition of expertise. The studies and domains differ substantially.

The chapter implication is simpler:

> Practice matters. It is not a complete theory of why people differ.

This is exactly the tone the book needs.

It allows us to reject both:

- 'talent is destiny';
- 'anyone can become anything with enough deliberate practice'.

Potential protected line:

> Practice can move the boundary. It does not make all boundaries disappear.

## 4. Component skills can improve without proving broad transfer

The strongest evidence for developability in this book comes from specific mechanisms already discussed.

Analogical encoding research shows that deliberately comparing cases can improve abstraction and later transfer in the studied negotiation settings (Loewenstein, Thompson & Gentner, 1999; Gentner, Loewenstein & Thompson, 2003).

That supports the trainability of a relevant component.

It does not prove a general increase in polymathic capability.

The distinction is central to Chapter 7:

> Improvement in a component is not the same thing as far transfer into a general capability.

## 5. Far transfer is the graveyard of easy training claims

Working-memory training provides an important cautionary example.

Melby-Lervåg, Redick and Hulme's meta-analytic review examined 87 publications with 145 experimental comparisons. Training reliably improved tasks that were identical or closely related to those practised, but there was no convincing evidence of reliable far-transfer gains in measures such as nonverbal ability, verbal ability, reading or arithmetic when compared with treated controls (Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme, 2016).

A more recent second-order meta-analysis similarly reports that transfer from working-memory training to broader abilities such as fluid intelligence and cognitive control is minimal, while effects are strongest on trained or closely related tasks.

This literature is not about polymathy directly.

Its value is methodological and conceptual:

> Training effects often shrink as the target moves further away from what was practised.

That should become one of Chapter 7's strongest safeguards.

Potential line:

> The farther the promised transfer, the stronger the evidence we should demand.

## 6. Developability should be framed as a portfolio, not a switch

The evidence now suggests a better architecture for the chapter.

Polymathic capability appears to involve a portfolio of components with different developmental profiles.

### Clearly learnable or improvable through ordinary education and practice

- domain knowledge and expertise;
- vocabulary and conceptual literacy in adjacent fields;
- methods for comparing examples;
- deliberate exposure to different domains;
- habits of questioning and explanation;
- explicit practices for checking analogies and competence boundaries.

### Supported as improvable in specific settings, but transfer should be bounded

- analogical encoding and transfer;
- some metacognitive and calibration practices;
- aspects of perspective shifting and flexible strategy use;
- some intentional personality change.

### Influenced by meaningful individual differences

- openness/intellect;
- general cognitive ability;
- motivation and persistence;
- prior educational opportunity;
- domain-specific aptitude;
- social and economic access to learning environments.

This is not a validated taxonomy. It is a provisional author synthesis for organising evidence.

## 7. The environment is not background noise

One risk in a chapter about individual development is pretending capability develops inside a sealed person.

Opportunity matters.

A curious person in a highly constrained environment may have fewer opportunities to develop breadth than someone with access to education, time, mentors, varied work and intellectual communities.

Conversely, environments can reinforce or suppress traits and habits over time.

The chapter should therefore avoid a purely self-development frame.

Potential line:

> Capability is personal, but development is ecological.

This is a strong candidate but should be tested for metaphor overload.

A plainer version:

> What people can develop depends partly on the environments in which they are able to learn and practise.

## 8. The book needs a distinction between capacity, capability and performance

A deeper conceptual hole is emerging.

A person may possess:

- underlying capacity or aptitude;
- developed capability through knowledge and practice;
- realised performance in a particular environment.

These are not interchangeable.

Someone may have high curiosity and reasoning ability but little opportunity to develop domain breadth.

Another person may have strong knowledge across fields but work in a system that rewards narrow role behaviour.

A third may perform polymathically in one context and not another because the problem, team or incentives differ.

This distinction could prevent Chapter 7 from becoming trait reductionism.

Do not formalise it as another signature framework unless the evidence and manuscript need it.

## 9. A more credible answer to 'Can I become a polymath?'

The chapter should refuse to answer the reader with a label.

The better answer is:

You can become more capable of polymathic thinking and work.

You can deepen an anchor domain.

You can broaden deliberately.

You can improve some forms of transfer through comparison and practice.

You can become more explicit about calibration and boundaries.

You can change some habits and perhaps some dispositional tendencies over time.

But there is no evidence that all people can be made equally polymathic, nor that a single training programme produces a general polymathic faculty.

Potential central line:

> **The useful question is not whether you can become a polymath. It is whether you can become more polymathically capable.**

This may be the governing line of the chapter.

## 10. Connection to Chapter 8

Chapter 7 should end with an evidence verdict, not a practice plan.

The conclusion should be:

- yes, meaningful development is possible;
- no, the capability is not infinitely plastic;
- no, component training should not be confused with broad transfer;
- individual differences and environments matter;
- therefore development should target practices and capabilities we have reason to believe can change.

That creates a natural handoff to Chapter 8:

> If development is possible but uneven, what should a serious polymathic practice actually train?

## Strong provisional claims

1. Personality traits show both stability and malleability across adulthood.
2. Openness has genetic and environmental influences and should not be treated as either fixed or freely trainable.
3. Deliberate practice contributes to performance differences but does not explain all variation, with effects differing substantially by domain.
4. Specific analogical-comparison interventions can improve abstraction and later transfer in studied settings.
5. Near transfer is generally easier to demonstrate than far transfer; claims of broad cognitive transfer require stronger evidence.
6. Working-memory training illustrates how gains on trained tasks can fail to generalise to broad cognitive abilities.
7. The evidence does not currently justify treating polymathic capability as one experimentally validated trainable trait.
8. A defensible developmental claim is that people can improve relevant component skills and practices, with outcomes shaped by individual differences and environment.

## Claims we should refuse to make

- Anyone can become a high-level polymath with sufficient effort.
- Polymathic capability is biologically fixed.
- Heritability implies immutability.
- Personality interventions reliably create large increases in openness.
- Deliberate practice explains expertise completely.
- Training cognitive flexibility automatically transfers to broad polymathic thinking.
- Improvement on one component demonstrates improvement in polymathy as a whole.
- A person's starting disposition places a hard ceiling on their development.

## Exploratory verdict

Chapter 7 should not ask whether polymathy is nature or nurture.

That framing is too crude.

The stronger question is:

> **Which ingredients are malleable, how far does improvement transfer, and what remains constrained by individual differences and environment?**

The emerging answer is deliberately asymmetric:

- development is real;
- transfer is limited;
- individual differences are real;
- environmental opportunity is real;
- none of these facts cancels the others.

The book can therefore defend a meaningful developmental promise without pretending human capability is infinitely plastic.

Status: strong first evidence pass. One further Mole pass should examine curiosity/openness more closely, metacognitive calibration training, and the distinction between capability development and performance opportunity before freezing the Final Brief.
