# Chapter 10 Evidence Build

## Collective Polymathy

Status: verified evidence architecture for drafting
Purpose: establish how distributed specialist depth can become coherent without collapsing differences, creating a universal integrator, or treating diversity itself as sufficient.

## Core evidence verdict

The evidence does not support a simple claim that more diverse expertise automatically produces better collective judgement.

The stronger pattern is conditional:

- teams benefit when members know where expertise resides;
- unique information must actually enter discussion;
- knowledge has to be translated and integrated rather than merely pooled;
- team boundaries must be actively managed;
- dissent can improve decisions when it increases information processing rather than merely conflict;
- psychological safety can support learning and voice, but it is not a substitute for expertise, information sharing or integration.

This supports the book-level distinction:

> **Distributed expertise is not yet collective polymathy.**

Collective polymathy requires mechanisms that make different forms of depth locatable, expressible, challengeable and integrable around a real problem.

## 1. Transactive memory: knowing who knows what

Transactive memory systems (TMS) describe a shared system through which members encode, store and retrieve differentiated knowledge by relying on one another's expertise rather than duplicating everything individually.

Bachrach et al.'s 2019 meta-analysis synthesised 76 empirical studies representing 6,869 sampling units and found a positive TMS-performance relationship whose strength varied with context (Bachrach et al., 2019).

A 2026 meta-analysis of 44 laboratory studies and 103 effect sizes found a moderate positive relationship between TMS and team outcomes (r = .44), while also showing that measured effect sizes varied substantially by outcome measurement method (Fausett et al., 2026).

Recent field evidence reinforces the mechanism. A 2025 study of hospital trauma teams linked stronger behavioural indicators of TMS with shorter ICU and hospital stays across 121 patient cases (Kahn et al., 2025). Another 2025 experiment in cross-functional strategic performance-system design found that TMS and team learning formed part of the pathway linking team identification to better design performance.

The chapter implication is narrow and strong:

> Teams do not need every member to know everything. They need reliable shared knowledge of where relevant expertise resides and how to access it.

Do not overstate TMS as a guarantee of performance. Context, task, routines and measurement matter.

## 2. Information sharing is necessary but common information dominates

Mesmer-Magnus and DeChurch's meta-analysis of 72 independent studies covering 4,795 groups and 17,279 participants found that information sharing positively predicted team performance, cohesion, decision satisfaction and knowledge integration, with important moderators (Mesmer-Magnus & DeChurch, 2009).

The hidden-profile literature exposes a sharper problem.

Lu, Yuan and McLeod's meta-analysis of 65 hidden-profile studies (101 independent effects; 3,189 groups) found that groups discussed substantially more common than unique information. Hidden-profile groups were eight times less likely to identify the correct solution than groups in which all relevant information was shared from the outset. Greater pooling of unique information was positively related to decision quality (Lu, Yuan & McLeod, 2012).

This supports a key Chapter 10 claim:

> **Having unique knowledge in the room is not the same as making it available to the decision.**

This may be one of the most important evidence anchors in the chapter.

It prevents a shallow 'diverse experts = better team' argument.

## 3. Dissent can improve information processing

Hidden-profile research also shows that disagreement can sometimes improve collective judgement because it changes how information is processed.

Schulz-Hardt et al. studied 135 three-person groups working on a hidden-profile personnel-selection task. Groups with homogeneous but incorrect initial preferences rarely solved the problem, while groups with prediscussion dissent achieved higher solution rates. The effect was mediated mainly by more intensive discussion and partly by reduced discussion bias (Schulz-Hardt et al., 2006).

This supports a bounded proposition:

> Dissent can be epistemically useful when it increases examination of information and weakens premature consensus.

Do not generalise this into 'conflict is good'.

The wider team-conflict literature is mixed, and relationship conflict is often damaging.

The relevant distinction for this book is between disagreement that opens the information space and conflict that degrades collaboration.

## 4. Psychological safety matters, but it is not the engine by itself

Frazier et al.'s meta-analysis combined 136 independent samples covering more than 22,000 individuals and nearly 5,000 groups and found meaningful relationships between psychological safety and workplace outcomes including task performance and citizenship behaviours, alongside identified antecedents and contextual variation (Frazier et al., 2017).

Newman, Donohue and Eva's systematic review likewise concluded that psychological safety is associated with important workplace outcomes while identifying measurement and theoretical gaps (Newman, Donohue & Eva, 2017).

For Chapter 10, psychological safety should be used narrowly:

- it can help create conditions in which people speak, question, admit uncertainty and challenge assumptions;
- it does not itself supply unique information;
- it does not integrate knowledge;
- it does not make dissent accurate;
- it does not eliminate status, expertise or coordination problems.

Potential protected line:

> **Safety can make it easier to speak. It does not decide whether what is spoken is useful.**

This is author synthesis.

Do not turn Chapter 10 into a psychological-safety chapter.

## 5. Boundary spanning links the team to expertise and resources beyond itself

Faraj and Yan's study of 64 software-development teams developed an open-systems model of boundary work including boundary spanning, buffering and reinforcement. These activities related to team performance and psychological safety, with effects moderated by task uncertainty and resource scarcity (Faraj & Yan, 2009).

A much larger 2025 meta-analysis of 85 primary studies covering 10,848 teams found an overall positive association between team boundary management and performance. Benefits were stronger for boundary-spanning activities such as coordination, representation and information search than for boundary-strengthening activities, and effects depended on who performed the activity and where the boundary was targeted (Leicht-Deobald et al., 2025).

This gives Chapter 10 a strong external-facing mechanism:

> A collectively polymathic team cannot assume all relevant depth is already inside the team.

Boundary spanning is how the team searches for, coordinates with and incorporates expertise beyond its existing membership.

Do not present boundary spanning as universally beneficial; the meta-analysis is explicitly contingent.

## 6. Boundary objects can help knowledge integration without requiring identical mental models

Caccamo, Pittino and Tell's 2023 systematic review of boundary objects and knowledge integration in innovation management concludes that boundary objects can function as interfaces through which diverse actors coordinate and integrate knowledge across disciplinary, functional and organisational boundaries (Caccamo, Pittino & Tell, 2023).

This is especially useful for the chapter because it offers a mechanism other than 'everyone must understand everyone else fully'.

Examples of boundary objects can include prototypes, diagrams, models, shared artefacts, maps or other representations that different specialists can work around while retaining distinct expertise.

Chapter implication:

> Integration does not require complete cognitive convergence.

A team can coordinate around a shared artefact while members continue to interpret it through different professional lenses.

Do not overclaim that boundary objects always integrate successfully; the review notes a heterogeneous and still-developing literature.

## 7. Diversity creates informational potential, not guaranteed performance

Van Knippenberg's 2024 review concludes that team diversity can generate synergy through exchange and integration of diverse perspectives, while also creating social-categorisation dynamics that can disrupt collaboration. The evidence is consistent with both pathways rather than a simple main-effect story (van Knippenberg, 2024).

A 2024 study of 215 knowledge-work teams similarly treats information elaboration — exchange, discussion and integration of task-relevant information — as the core process through which informational resources can contribute to innovation. Crucially, functional diversity and external information resources did not automatically produce elaboration; effects depended on team conditions and shared understandings (van Knippenberg et al., 2024).

This supports a central Chapter 10 boundary:

> **Diversity supplies difference. Collective polymathy requires integration.**

Do not imply demographic diversity and expertise diversity are interchangeable. Keep the book primarily focused on diversity of relevant knowledge, expertise and perspective while acknowledging that team diversity research broadly shows contingent effects.

## 8. Integration is not flattening

The evidence across TMS, information sharing, boundary objects and knowledge integration suggests a deeper book-level synthesis.

Effective collective work does not require every specialist to become a generalist.

Nor does it require the group to dissolve differences into one shared view.

Instead, the team needs enough shared orientation to:

- locate relevant expertise;
- make unique knowledge visible;
- translate key distinctions;
- test disagreement;
- combine implications;
- preserve specialist authority where depth matters.

This supports an original proposition:

> **The goal is not shared expertise. It is shared access to differentiated expertise.**

This is author synthesis, not an established construct.

## 9. Coordination routines matter

A 2026 study of 24 teams in a dynamic crisis-management task found that TMS did not have a direct effect on performance; its effect operated indirectly through patterns of interaction between interdependent roles. Action patterns involving only one role did not improve performance (current citation details to verify before manuscript lock).

This is useful because it prevents the chapter from treating awareness of expertise as sufficient.

Knowing who knows what must translate into coordinated action.

Potential synthesis:

> **Collective intelligence is not stored only in people. Some of it lives in the routines that connect them.**

Use cautiously and label as author interpretation.

## 10. The chapter's evidence hierarchy

### Strong evidence-backed claims

1. Transactive memory systems are positively associated with team outcomes, though effects vary by context and measurement.
2. Information sharing is positively related to team performance and knowledge integration.
3. Groups systematically privilege shared information over unique information in hidden-profile tasks.
4. Pooling unique information is associated with better hidden-profile decision quality.
5. Prediscussion dissent can improve hidden-profile decision quality under some conditions by increasing information processing.
6. Psychological safety is associated with important team/work outcomes and can support voice and learning conditions.
7. Boundary management is positively associated with team performance overall, with important contingencies.
8. Boundary objects can support knowledge integration across specialised groups.
9. Diversity effects on performance and innovation are contingent; informational diversity is not automatically converted into value.

### Author synthesis supported by the evidence

- Distributed expertise is not yet collective polymathy.
- Collective polymathy requires expertise to be locatable, expressible, challengeable and integrable.
- The goal is shared access to differentiated expertise, not shared expertise.
- Safety can make it easier to speak; it does not determine whether the contribution is useful.
- A team can become collectively broader without making every member individually broad.

## Claims we should refuse to make

- Diverse teams automatically outperform homogeneous teams.
- Psychological safety guarantees good decisions.
- More dissent always improves performance.
- Conflict is inherently beneficial.
- TMS guarantees team performance.
- Information sharing alone equals knowledge integration.
- Every team needs a single polymathic integrator.
- All specialists must understand one another's domains deeply.
- Boundary spanning is always beneficial.
- Collective polymathy is identical to collective intelligence.
- AI can replace the social and professional processes by which specialist knowledge is challenged and integrated.

## Drafting architecture suggested by the evidence

The chapter should not be structured as 'features of high-performing teams'.

A stronger argument is:

**one person cannot carry all relevant depth -> distribute expertise -> know who knows what -> surface unique information -> preserve useful dissent -> translate across boundaries -> integrate through shared artefacts/routines -> reach beyond the team when needed -> retain specialist authority and collective accountability.**

This keeps Collective Polymathy distinct from generic teamwork.

## Visual implication

The visual opportunity is strong.

Do not draw a circle of happy specialists.

Preferred concept:

- several specialist nodes with visibly different depth;
- transactive awareness links showing who knows what;
- a shared problem or artefact at the centre;
- translation and challenge across boundaries;
- external boundary-spanning links where expertise is missing;
- an integrated decision/output emerging without erasing specialist differences.
