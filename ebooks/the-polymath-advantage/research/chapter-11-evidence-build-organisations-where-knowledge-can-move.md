# Chapter 11 Evidence Build

## Organisations Where Knowledge Can Move

Status: verified evidence architecture for drafting
Purpose: establish how organisational design, knowledge flows, absorptive capacity, exploration/exploitation, network structure and incentives affect whether polymathic capability can operate above the team level.

## Core evidence verdict

Chapter 10 established that differentiated specialist depth becomes collectively useful only when it can be located, surfaced, translated, challenged and integrated around a shared problem.

Chapter 11 moves one level higher.

The organisational question is not simply whether knowledgeable people exist or whether teams collaborate well. It is whether the organisation creates conditions in which knowledge can move to the places where it becomes relevant.

The strongest evidence does not support a single best organisational structure.

Instead, several contingent mechanisms recur:

- organisations need the capacity to recognise, assimilate and use knowledge that originates outside existing routines;
- knowledge transfer depends on network structure and the relationships connecting sources and recipients;
- exploration and exploitation create real but context-dependent tensions;
- structures that help exploration in one setting may hinder it in another;
- incentives can encourage knowledge sharing but can also produce hiding, competition or gaming;
- more accessible information is useful only when the organisation can absorb and apply it.

This supports the book-level proposition:

> **An organisation can possess enormous knowledge and still be poor at moving knowledge to where it matters.**

That is author synthesis.

## 1. Absorptive capacity: access is not enough

Absorptive capacity provides one of the strongest foundations for Chapter 11.

The concept refers to an organisation's ability to recognise valuable external knowledge, assimilate or transform it and use it productively. Its classic roots lie in Cohen and Levinthal, with later extensions including Zahra and George.

A particularly useful current meta-analysis is Stettler et al. (2025). The study synthesised 145 empirical studies covering 434,985 firms and 798,650 firm-year observations. It found a positive relationship between absorptive capacity and innovation, with substantial contextual variation. The relationship was stronger in knowledge-rich environments, and the authors report a larger effect in the smartphone era than in the preinternet/early-internet period (Stettler et al., 2025).

This result is highly relevant after Chapter 9.

AI and digital connectivity can dramatically increase the amount of accessible external knowledge. That does not mean every organisation benefits equally. The capacity to absorb and use knowledge becomes more important as knowledge availability rises.

Potential synthesis:

> **The more knowledge becomes available, the more valuable the ability to absorb it becomes.**

This is an author-level inference from the meta-analysis, not a direct quote or universal law.

Do not equate absorptive capacity with Collective Polymathy. It is an established organisational-learning construct that provides one mechanism relevant to the book's theory.

## 2. Knowledge transfer is shaped by organisational and network conditions

Knowledge does not move automatically because someone publishes it, stores it or sends it.

Research on organisational knowledge transfer identifies characteristics of the knowledge itself, the source and recipient, organisational context and network structure as relevant to transfer success.

Stettler et al. summarise earlier meta-analytic evidence from van Wijk, Jansen and Lyles showing positive relationships between absorptive capacity and organisational knowledge transfer, and between knowledge transfer and both innovation and firm performance.

Argote and colleagues' work also emphasises that knowledge transfer depends on the interaction of members, tasks, tools and networks rather than on information availability alone.

A review of organisational knowledge transfer reports Reagans and McEvily's finding that both network cohesion and network range supported faster knowledge transfer beyond the effect of tie strength, with tie strength particularly relevant to tacit knowledge.

Chapter implication:

> The architecture of relationships affects whether knowledge can travel.

Do not reduce this to 'more networking is better'. Dense cohesive ties and wider-ranging connections can perform different functions; knowledge complexity and tacitness matter.

## 3. Silos are not merely structural boxes

The word silo is commonly used too loosely.

A formal department is not automatically a dysfunctional silo. Specialisation requires boundaries. Chapter 3 and Chapter 10 have already established why depth and differentiation matter.

The problem begins when organisational boundaries interrupt useful knowledge movement.

For Chapter 11, define a dysfunctional silo behaviourally rather than architecturally:

- relevant knowledge remains trapped;
- functions optimise locally while missing cross-system consequences;
- expertise is difficult to locate or access;
- incentives reward withholding or territoriality;
- translation costs become high enough that cross-boundary inquiry stops.

The knowledge-hiding literature provides stronger evidence than generic commentary about silos.

Shen et al. (2025) meta-analysed 267 independent samples from 248 studies and found significant relationships between knowledge hiding and leadership, HR practices, personality factors and both behavioural/psychological and performance-related outcomes.

Arain et al.'s earlier meta-analysis synthesised 104 studies representing 31,822 employees and established a broad nomological network around knowledge hiding.

The safe Chapter 11 claim is:

> Organisational conditions can make people less willing to make useful knowledge available, and knowledge hiding is associated with poorer performance-related outcomes.

Do not say departmental structures themselves cause knowledge hiding.

## 4. Incentives matter, but simple reward logic is dangerous

The organisation may say 'share knowledge' while rewarding people for guarding it.

The evidence is mixed enough that Chapter 11 should avoid simplistic prescriptions.

Kovach's 2026 review of recent organisational knowledge-sharing literature shows that reward systems can have different effects depending on design and social context. Some studies find group incentives can support sharing, while relative performance information and status-linked incentives can encourage knowledge withholding or hiding.

A 2023 study of 138 business-unit management teams found that in-team clan control and monitoring were associated with team performance through knowledge sharing, while relative emphasis on team-based incentives was not directly associated in the same way. The interactions among controls mattered (Kachelmeier et al. / full authors to verify before citation if used).

This supports a bounded proposition:

> **What organisations reward changes what people are willing to make visible.**

But incentives are not a control knob with a single direction. Monetary, status, team and relative-performance systems can interact differently with knowledge-sharing motives.

Do not promise that team incentives solve silos.

## 5. Exploration and exploitation are both necessary but structurally difficult

Chapter 8 used exploration and consolidation at the individual level. Chapter 11 needs the organisational version carefully distinguished from that metaphor.

Organisational-learning research distinguishes exploration — experimentation, search and new alternatives — from exploitation — refinement, efficiency and extension of existing competence.

Junni et al.'s meta-analysis found positive organisational ambidexterity–performance relationships but also substantial moderation by context and research design (Junni et al., 2013).

Fourné et al.'s 2019 meta-analysis of 114 primary studies covering 33,492 organisations found that structural and contextual approaches to balancing exploration and exploitation work differently depending on firm size and industry. Structural separation was more conducive in some high-technology contexts, while service firms could benefit differently (Fourné et al., 2019).

Wenke, Zapkau and Schwens' meta-analysis of 34 studies and 5,488 SMEs found that exploration, exploitation and ambidexterity were all positively related to SME performance, but SMEs benefited more from exploration or exploitation than from ambidexterity in their sample (Wenke, Zapkau & Schwens, 2021).

The implication is not 'ambidexterity is best'.

It is:

> **Organisations need ways to search beyond what they already know without losing the ability to execute what they know well.**

How that balance is organised is contingent.

## 6. Structure should follow the knowledge problem

The ambidexterity evidence prevents a fashionable but weak claim that organisations should always eliminate silos, flatten hierarchy or create fluid networks.

Specialisation, boundaries and dedicated units can be useful.

The more defensible design principle is:

> **Do not remove boundaries indiscriminately. Make important boundaries permeable where the work requires knowledge to cross them.**

This is author synthesis.

Some exploratory work may benefit from looser coupling, autonomy or structural separation. Some exploitative work benefits from stable routines, standardisation and clear authority.

Chapter 11 should therefore resist one-size-fits-all organisational design rhetoric.

The issue is not whether an organisation is hierarchical, matrixed, networked or agile in the abstract.

The issue is whether the structure helps relevant knowledge reach consequential decisions without destroying accountability or specialist depth.

## 7. Organisational range needs absorptive depth

One of the strongest connections across the book emerges here.

Chapter 3 established that individual breadth is not enough without depth.

At organisational level, external partnerships, large networks, AI access and broad scanning can create extraordinary range. But an organisation may still lack enough prior knowledge, expertise or integration capability to recognise which external information matters.

Stettler et al. (2025) directly supports the broad absorptive-capacity/innovation relationship and its dependence on knowledge environment.

Solís-Molina, Hernández-Espallardo and Rodríguez-Orejuela (2018) found in 281 manufacturing firms that absorptive capacity moderated whether organisational ambidexterity or specialisation was more strongly associated with performance.

This reinforces a book-level symmetry:

> **Organisations, like individuals, cannot rely on range alone. They need enough internal depth to recognise, challenge and use what arrives from outside.**

This is synthesis, not a direct finding of either study.

## 8. Knowledge abundance can increase the importance of selection

This is where Chapter 11 reconnects directly with Chapter 9.

AI, digital platforms and global connectivity make it easier for organisations to gather research, market signals, expert opinion, customer data and competitor intelligence.

The danger is assuming that greater inflow produces greater organisational intelligence.

Stettler et al.'s current meta-analysis is particularly useful because it shows stronger absorptive-capacity effects in knowledge-rich environments.

The organisational bottleneck therefore shifts from simple acquisition toward:

- recognition;
- interpretation;
- prioritisation;
- integration;
- application.

Potential line:

> **An organisation can drown in access while starving for understanding.**

Author synthesis.

This should not become another AI chapter. AI is one intensifier of the knowledge-abundance problem, not the chapter's subject.

## 9. Knowledge movement depends on both formal and informal systems

Formal structures matter:

- reporting lines;
- decision rights;
- team architecture;
- governance forums;
- knowledge repositories;
- review processes.

Informal structures matter too:

- trusted relationships;
- professional communities;
- informal advice networks;
- boundary-spanning individuals;
- dormant ties that can be reactivated.

Knowledge-transfer research indicates that network cohesion, range and tie strength affect transfer under different conditions.

This gives Chapter 11 another useful distinction:

> **The organisation chart shows authority. It does not show the whole knowledge system.**

Author synthesis.

Do not romanticise informal networks. They can also create exclusion, bottlenecks and dependence on key individuals.

## 10. The organisation should not require a central universal integrator

Chapter 10 rejected the idea that one team integrator must understand every specialist.

The same principle applies at organisational scale.

An organisation does not become polymathically capable by hiring a small group of unusually broad executives who personally translate everything.

Knowledge integration can live in:

- cross-functional forums;
- boundary roles;
- communities of practice;
- shared artefacts and standards;
- rotating project teams;
- escalation pathways;
- digital systems;
- governance routines;
- trusted external networks.

Chapter 11 should explore these as examples, not as a prescribed organisational operating model.

Potential synthesis:

> **The organisation needs connective capacity, not a single connective genius.**

This is a direct extension of the book's Chapter 2 and Chapter 10 logic.

## 11. Organisational polymathic capability is not 'knowledge management'

The chapter must distinguish itself from a generic knowledge-management text.

Knowledge management often concerns capturing, storing, sharing and using knowledge.

The narrower concern here is how an organisation remains capable of:

- maintaining specialist depth;
- locating relevant expertise;
- bringing external and internal knowledge into contact;
- reframing problems across functional boundaries;
- integrating without flattening;
- recognising organisational competence boundaries;
- balancing search with execution.

The proposed book-level idea is not that all knowledge should flow everywhere.

That would create noise, overload and weakened accountability.

The goal is **selective permeability**: relevant knowledge should be able to cross the boundaries that would otherwise prevent a sound decision.

## 12. Strong evidence-backed claims safe for drafting

1. Absorptive capacity is positively associated with innovation, with meaningful contextual variation.
2. The effect of absorptive capacity is stronger in some knowledge-rich environments, making knowledge availability and organisational absorption distinct variables.
3. Organisational knowledge transfer depends on characteristics of the knowledge, organisational context and network relationships.
4. Knowledge hiding is associated with organisational, leadership and HR antecedents and with negative performance-related outcomes.
5. Incentives and management controls can influence knowledge-sharing behaviour, but effects depend on design and context.
6. Exploration, exploitation and organisational ambidexterity have performance relationships that vary by context.
7. Structural and contextual approaches to ambidexterity are contingent rather than universally superior.
8. Network cohesion, range and tie strength can support different aspects of knowledge transfer.
9. Greater access to external knowledge does not guarantee that an organisation can recognise, integrate or use it.

## Claims we should refuse to make

- Silos are always bad.
- Flat organisations share knowledge better than hierarchical ones.
- Matrix organisations are inherently more polymathic.
- Agile structures automatically improve knowledge flow.
- Team incentives always improve knowledge sharing.
- More information always improves organisational decisions.
- Absorptive capacity guarantees innovation.
- Ambidexterity is always superior to specialisation.
- Informal networks are always beneficial.
- AI solves organisational knowledge silos.
- Every piece of knowledge should be visible to everyone.
- Collective polymathy is simply another name for knowledge management.

## Drafting architecture suggested by the evidence

The chapter should not become 'how to build an innovative organisation'.

The stronger sequence is:

**knowledge exists -> organisational boundaries organise depth -> boundaries can also block movement -> absorptive capacity determines what external knowledge becomes usable -> networks and incentives affect transfer -> exploration and exploitation need different conditions -> organisations need selective permeability rather than boundarylessness -> AI increases knowledge abundance -> organisational polymathic capability depends on making the right knowledge movable without making everything diffuse.**

## Visual opportunity

Visual opportunity: YES.

Preferred concept: **The Organisational Knowledge System**.

Do not draw a traditional organisation chart.

Possible structure:

- specialist functions as deep vertical domains;
- a consequential problem crossing several domains;
- selective cross-boundary channels showing knowledge movement;
- external knowledge flowing into the system;
- absorptive capacity as the organisational ability to recognise/integrate useful external knowledge;
- exploration zones and exploitation/routine zones shown as different but connected operating modes;
- decision/governance nodes preserving accountability;
- AI as one accelerant of external access, not the central object.

Key visual principle:

> **Boundaries preserve depth. Permeability allows relevant knowledge to move.**
