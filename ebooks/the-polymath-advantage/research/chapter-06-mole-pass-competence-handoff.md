# Chapter 6 Mole Pass

## Competence Boundaries, Expert Disagreement and the Calibrated Handoff

Status: targeted exploration addendum
Purpose: deepen the Chapter 6 theory before freezing the Final Brief.

## 1. The handoff is not a retreat

The previous exploration established that polymathic judgement requires knowing when another person's depth must take over.

This can sound like a retreat from polymathy if framed poorly.

It is not.

A person who can identify the relevant domain, recognise the limits of their own understanding, locate the right specialist and ask questions that improve the joint judgement is doing something intellectually sophisticated.

The competence boundary is therefore not merely a stopping point.

It can also be a routing decision.

Potential line:

> The mature polymath does not need to contain every answer. They need to know when the answer has moved beyond them and where deeper expertise lives.

## 2. Transactive memory gives us a neighbouring evidence base

Research on transactive memory systems is useful here because it studies how groups distribute knowledge and develop shared awareness of 'who knows what'.

Peltokorpi's 2019 review describes transactive memory systems as involving communication through which group members learn who possesses expertise in different domains, specialise, and retrieve information from domain experts. Commonly studied features include specialisation, credibility and coordination (Peltokorpi, 2019).

Argote and colleagues similarly describe transactive memory as a collective system in which members know who possesses specialised knowledge and can route questions or tasks accordingly (Argote & Ren, 2012).

We should not import the whole team-level theory into Chapter 6. Later chapters on collective polymathy own that territory.

But it gives strong support to a narrow idea:

> Knowing who knows what is itself part of effective knowledge use.

This strengthens the calibrated-handoff argument without implying that individuals must internalise every relevant domain.

## 3. The handoff has three parts

Not a formal model yet, but the evidence suggests a useful practical sequence.

### Recognition

I know enough to recognise that this problem has crossed into another expertise domain.

### Selection

I can identify whose expertise is relevant and distinguish credible specialist input from generic opinion.

### Translation

I can frame the question well enough that the specialist can see the decision context, while I can understand enough of the response to integrate it back into the broader problem.

The third part is especially important.

A handoff without translation becomes dumping.

A leader who says 'the lawyers will handle it' or 'let IT decide' has not necessarily exercised polymathic judgement. The wider decision still requires integration.

Potential protected line:

> Handoff is not abdication. Someone still has to reconnect the specialist answer to the whole problem.

## 4. Expert disagreement complicates deference

Another important hole: what happens when specialists disagree?

A simplistic competence-boundary rule would say:

Ask the expert and defer.

But real professional life often contains expert disagreement.

Philosophical work on epistemic peer disagreement is useful as a caution. Pritchard argues that intellectual humility does not automatically require conciliation or lowering one's confidence merely because another epistemic peer disagrees (Pritchard, 2021).

This is not an empirical decision rule for executives, and Chapter 6 should not become an epistemology seminar.

Its practical value is narrower:

> Intellectual humility is not automatic surrender to whoever carries the expert label.

If credible experts disagree, the polymathic task changes.

The leader may need to inspect:

- whether they are answering the same question;
- whether they are using different evidence;
- whether the disagreement comes from values, assumptions, models or facts;
- whether one expert's domain extends further into the decision than another's;
- what uncertainty remains even after specialist input.

Potential line:

> Expertise deserves weight. It does not eliminate judgement.

## 5. Credibility matters as much as locating expertise

Transactive-memory research also warns us that 'who knows what' is not enough. Teams rely on credibility as well as specialisation.

This matters because modern information environments make apparent expertise easy to perform.

The calibrated handoff is therefore not:

Find someone who sounds specialist.

It is:

Find expertise whose depth is relevant, credible and appropriately matched to the question.

Do not overdevelop credentialism. Formal qualifications can matter greatly in regulated professions, but competence can also come from experience, research, practice and context-specific knowledge.

The chapter should focus on fit between expertise and decision.

## 6. Boundary spanning without boundary erasure

Faraj and Yan's study of knowledge teams found that teams engage in several kinds of boundary work, including boundary spanning and boundary reinforcement, and that these activities relate to team performance under different conditions (Faraj & Yan, 2009).

This is conceptually useful because polymathic thinking needs both movements.

We cross boundaries to access knowledge.

We sometimes reinforce boundaries to protect quality, accountability and specialised judgement.

That is an important counterweight to simplistic interdisciplinarity.

Potential line:

> Good boundary spanning does not require boundary erasure.

This may become one of Chapter 6's central ideas.

## 7. A stronger definition of polymathic judgement is emerging

The initial definition was:

> Polymathic judgement is the capacity to use knowledge across domains while calibrating confidence, recognising competence boundaries and knowing when another person's depth must take over.

The handoff research suggests one refinement:

> **Polymathic judgement is the capacity to integrate knowledge across domains while calibrating confidence, preserving competence boundaries and routing decisions to deeper expertise when the limits of one's own understanding are reached.**

This remains author synthesis.

The word 'routing' is valuable because it makes judgement active rather than merely humble.

But we should test whether it sounds too managerial in the final brief.

## 8. Three failure modes now appear distinct

Chapter 6 may become clearer if it differentiates three kinds of failure.

### Transfer failure

The analogy or imported concept does not actually fit the target.

### Calibration failure

The person over- or underestimates how much they understand or how reliable their judgement is.

### Handoff failure

The person either fails to involve deeper expertise when needed, or defers so completely that integration and accountability disappear.

This distinction is promising but should remain descriptive unless further evidence supports a formal framework.

Potential protected line:

> Knowing when to stop is not enough. You also need to know what happens next.

## 9. The deeper book implication

The polymathic ideal is changing.

It is no longer the individual who contains the most fields.

It is increasingly the person who can:

- recognise relevant structures across domains;
- judge the strength of the transfer;
- know where their own competence ends;
- locate and interrogate deeper expertise;
- integrate that expertise back into the wider decision.

This begins to connect individual polymathy with later collective polymathy without collapsing the distinction.

The individual capability is partly **meta-expertise**: knowledge about where knowledge resides, what kind of expertise the problem requires and how specialist contributions fit together.

Use 'meta-expertise' cautiously; do not add it as another signature construct unless necessary.

A plainer formulation may be stronger:

> Part of knowing more is knowing who knows more than you.

## 10. Final exploratory verdict

The extra Mole pass strengthens Chapter 6 substantially.

The chapter should not end with 'know your limits'. That is too passive and too familiar.

The stronger proposition is:

> **Polymathic judgement is calibrated movement across expertise boundaries, including the judgement to hand off without handing away responsibility.**

The chapter can now connect:

- illusion of understanding;
- confidence calibration;
- intellectual humility;
- competence boundaries;
- credible expertise;
- expert disagreement;
- calibrated handoff.

This produces a more complete answer to the original danger of polymathy: breadth does not become trustworthy merely because someone knows their limits. Trustworthy cross-domain judgement also requires knowing how to work with depth they do not personally possess.
