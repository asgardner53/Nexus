# Chapter 7 Mole Pass

## Curiosity, Calibration and the Conditions for Expression

Status: targeted exploration addendum
Purpose: resolve three outstanding questions before freezing the Chapter 7 Final Brief: curiosity/openness, calibration training and the distinction between developed capability and realised performance.

## 1. Curiosity is relevant, but it is not one thing

Curiosity is tempting to use as a shorthand explanation for polymathic development. That would be too simple.

Kashdan and colleagues' Five-Dimensional Curiosity Scale research argues that curiosity is multidimensional rather than a single continuum from incurious to curious. Their 2018 work identified distinct dimensions including Joyous Exploration, Deprivation Sensitivity, Stress Tolerance, Social Curiosity and Thrill Seeking, with different profiles and correlates (Kashdan et al., 2018). The revised measure later separated overt and covert social curiosity while retaining the broader multidimensional logic (Kashdan et al., 2020).

The implication for this book is important:

> A person can be highly curious in one way without being broadly exploratory in every way.

A relentless problem solver, a socially curious interviewer and someone who actively seeks unfamiliar experiences may all be 'curious', but the developmental pathways they support are not identical.

This weakens any claim that one curiosity trait explains polymathic capability.

It strengthens a subtler proposition: different forms of curiosity may create different opportunities for breadth, depth and exploration.

## 2. Curiosity and openness should not be collapsed

Curiosity overlaps conceptually and empirically with broader personality dimensions such as openness/intellect, but it should not simply be treated as another name for openness.

For Chapter 7, the safest position is:

- openness/intellect describes relatively broad patterns of receptivity to ideas, novelty, imagination and intellectual engagement;
- curiosity is a motivational tendency to seek information, resolve uncertainty or explore novelty;
- the constructs overlap but are not interchangeable.

This matters because the developmental question changes depending on what we mean.

A person may not dramatically alter a broad personality trait yet may still deliberately increase the frequency with which they ask questions, enter unfamiliar domains, compare perspectives or seek information gaps.

That is a crucial distinction for the book's promise.

Potential line:

> You do not need to become a different personality before you can practise a different pattern of inquiry.

This is author synthesis and should not be presented as an experimentally established claim about personality change.

## 3. Environment changes whether curiosity pays off

Kashdan and Yuen's 2007 study of 484 Hong Kong adolescents tested interactions between trait curiosity and perceived school quality. Highly curious students in more challenging school environments showed the strongest academic outcomes, while highly curious students in less challenging perceived environments showed weaker outcomes (Kashdan & Yuen, 2007).

The study is specific to adolescents, schooling and perceived academic environment. It should not be generalised directly to workplaces or adult polymathy.

But it supports a broader interactionist idea: the value expressed by a trait can depend on whether the environment provides relevant opportunities and demands.

Trait Activation Theory provides a stronger organisational foundation for this point. Tett, Toich and Ozkum's 2021 review describes personality traits as being expressed in response to trait-relevant situational cues, subject to constraints and facilitators at task, social and organisational levels (Tett, Toich & Ozkum, 2021).

This gives Chapter 7 an important distinction:

> **Possessing a tendency is not the same as having an environment that allows the tendency to become useful behaviour.**

A broad, curious professional in a role with no autonomy, no cross-functional exposure and strong penalties for exploration may display less polymathic behaviour than the same person in a role that rewards integration and inquiry.

This does not mean the environment creates the underlying disposition from nothing. It means realised performance depends partly on the opportunities, constraints and cues surrounding the person.

## 4. Capability and performance are not the same thing

The environment evidence strengthens a distinction already emerging in the first exploration.

A useful, non-formal distinction is:

- **capacity**: underlying aptitudes and dispositions that affect what may be possible;
- **capability**: developed knowledge, skills and practices a person can bring to a problem;
- **performance**: what is actually expressed in a particular situation.

This is author synthesis, not a validated three-part model.

Its practical value is substantial.

A person may possess strong intellectual curiosity but little developed cross-domain knowledge.

Another may have broad knowledge and strong integrative capability but work in a role designed around narrow procedural compliance.

A third may display sophisticated polymathic performance only when a problem genuinely requires several domains to be connected.

The distinction protects us from mistaking observed behaviour for total potential.

Potential protected line:

> **Capability belongs to the person. Performance belongs to the person-in-context.**

This line is conceptually strong but should be tested against the evidence wording before manuscript use.

## 5. Calibration can improve in some tasks without becoming a general skill

The calibration-training literature reinforces the chapter's transfer warning.

Kelly and Mandel's 2024 study tested a commercial calibration course with 70 intelligence analysts. Overall miscalibration declined, but the effect differed by task. Analysts improved on interval-estimation calibration while becoming more underconfident on a binary-choice task. Changes across the two tasks were uncorrelated. The authors concluded that the training appeared to shift confidence downward rather than clearly improving a general metacognitive monitoring ability (Kelly & Mandel, 2024).

This is almost tailor-made for Chapter 7.

It shows why a successful intervention result on one task should not automatically be called a general increase in calibration skill.

A later study by Martin tested performance-feedback calibration training in two large experiments and found no improvement in calibration from the tested intervention (Martin, 2025).

Together these studies support a cautious formulation:

> Calibration is not obviously a domain-general skill that can be upgraded once and carried everywhere.

That does not mean calibration cannot improve. Feedback, training and structured forecasting practice have improved calibration in some settings. It means the mechanism and transfer boundary matter.

Potential line:

> **A training effect is not a portable trait until transfer has been demonstrated.**

This may be one of the chapter's strongest methodological safeguards.

## 6. Underconfidence is also miscalibration

The calibration literature gives the book another useful correction.

Many popular discussions treat overconfidence as the only problem and humility as the cure.

Kelly and Mandel's findings show why that is inadequate. Analysts who were already underconfident could become even more underconfident after an intervention oriented partly toward reducing confidence.

This strengthens Chapter 6's claim and Chapter 7's developmental logic:

> The aim is not less confidence. It is confidence that tracks the quality of the judgement.

Developmental interventions that merely suppress confidence may create the appearance of humility while degrading calibration in people who were already cautious.

## 7. Curiosity itself can be situationally triggered

Recent educational research continues to treat curiosity not only as a trait but also as a state that can be elicited by novelty, uncertainty, ambiguity and knowledge gaps. A 2025 npj Science of Learning study, for example, examined triggers of epistemic curiosity during classroom discourse and reflects the broader view that curiosity can fluctuate with context as well as differ between people.

This should not be used as proof that durable trait curiosity can be trained.

Its value is more practical:

> Development does not require every relevant tendency to become a permanent personality trait. Some useful behaviours can be designed into situations and routines.

A person can create question prompts, comparison practices, cross-domain reading routines or project structures that repeatedly elicit exploratory behaviour even if their baseline disposition remains relatively stable.

This is a bridge into Chapter 8, not a complete training claim.

## 8. The chapter's developmental promise is becoming more precise

The evidence now supports four levels of developmental claim with different confidence.

### Strong claim

People can acquire knowledge, methods, vocabulary and domain expertise through learning and practice.

### Moderate claim

Specific practices can improve component performances such as analogical comparison, certain calibration tasks, information seeking or flexible problem-solving in defined contexts.

### Cautious claim

Some broader dispositional patterns can change over time and under intervention, but effect sizes, mechanisms and durability vary.

### Unsupported claim

A single training regime produces a durable, domain-general increase in a unified trait called polymathic capability.

This hierarchy may be useful in the Final Brief but should not necessarily appear as a boxed framework in the chapter.

## 9. The real answer to the founding question

The evidence now supports a more compelling answer than either nature or nurture.

> **Polymathic development is a person-by-practice-by-environment process.**

This is author synthesis and should not be elevated into a formal model.

Its meaning is straightforward:

- people differ in starting dispositions and aptitudes;
- practice and learning can change meaningful components;
- transfer is not guaranteed;
- environments determine which tendencies and capabilities are elicited, rewarded and sustained.

A simpler reader-facing formulation may be stronger:

> **What you can develop depends partly on what you bring, what you practise and where you get to practise it.**

## 10. Final Mole verdict

The second pass changes the Chapter 7 emphasis in three useful ways.

First, curiosity should be treated as multidimensional and context-sensitive rather than as the master trait of polymaths.

Second, calibration training becomes a concrete example of why component improvement cannot be assumed to generalise.

Third, the environment moves from a footnote to part of the developmental explanation. A person's potential, developed capability and realised performance are not the same thing.

This gives the chapter a much more credible central answer:

> **You can become more polymathically capable, but development is uneven, component-specific and context-dependent.**

The next chapter can then legitimately ask how to build a practice around the parts that are most plausibly developable.
