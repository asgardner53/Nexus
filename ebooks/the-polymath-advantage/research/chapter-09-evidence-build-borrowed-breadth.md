# Chapter 9 Evidence Build

## Borrowed Breadth

Status: verified evidence architecture for drafting
Purpose: support the Chapter 9 argument that generative AI can widen access and improve performance in some knowledge-work settings while introducing task-dependent error, overreliance and governance risks that make verification and judgement more important.

## Core evidence verdict

The strongest current evidence does not support either a simple augmentation story or a simple risk story.

Generative AI effects are heterogeneous across tasks and users.

In some settings, AI materially improves speed and quality, especially for less experienced workers. In other settings, access to AI can reduce the probability of a correct answer. Separate behavioural research also shows that people can over-rely on AI advice, including when it conflicts with available contextual information.

This makes heterogeneity itself the most useful evidence anchor for Borrowed Breadth.

## 1. AI can improve knowledge-work performance, but gains vary by worker and task

Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond's field study of 5,172 customer-support agents found that access to a generative AI conversational assistant increased productivity by around 15% on average. Effects were heterogeneous: less experienced and lower-skilled workers improved both speed and quality, while the most experienced and highest-skilled workers saw smaller gains in speed and small declines in quality. The final journal version was published in The Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2025 (Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, 2025).

This supports a bounded claim:

> AI can improve performance in some knowledge-work settings, with effects that differ substantially across workers.

It does not support a universal claim that AI narrows all expertise gaps or improves all professional work.

## 2. The 'jagged frontier' is a powerful Chapter 9 anchor

Dell'Acqua and colleagues studied 758 BCG consultants completing realistic knowledge-work tasks with and without GPT-4. On tasks selected to fall within the model's capability frontier, AI users completed more tasks, worked faster and produced higher-quality outputs. On a complex task selected to fall outside that frontier, however, AI users were 19 percentage points less likely to produce a correct solution than those without AI (Dell'Acqua et al., 2026; working-paper version first circulated in 2023).

The study is now published in Organization Science / Articles in Advance in 2026.

This is especially valuable because it directly demonstrates that apparent task similarity does not guarantee similar AI value.

Chapter implication:

> AI assistance can improve performance on one task and degrade it on another inside the same broad knowledge workflow.

This gives empirical backbone to the book-level line:

> The cost of asking across disciplines has collapsed. The cost of being right has not.

The aphorism remains author synthesis; the study supports the heterogeneity underneath it.

## 3. Overreliance is an empirically supported risk, not just an intuition

Klingbeil, Grützner and Schreck conducted an incentivised behavioural experiment on reliance on AI advice in uncertain decisions. Participants over-relied on AI advice even when it conflicted with available contextual information and their own assessment. The authors report that merely knowing advice was generated by AI increased reliance and that overreliance could produce inefficient outcomes for both participants and third parties (Klingbeil, Grützner & Schreck, 2024).

This supports a bounded claim:

> Under some conditions, people can over-rely on AI-generated advice even when contextual information should prompt scepticism.

A 2026 Scientific Reports study by Pearson and colleagues provides additional evidence that attitudes towards AI can affect reliance and decision quality. In a face-authenticity task with deliberately unreliable guidance, participants with more positive attitudes towards AI showed poorer discrimination when given AI guidance (Pearson et al., 2026).

Do not generalise either study to all professional settings.

Use them to support the narrower proposition that reliance quality is a human-AI interaction problem rather than something solved merely by placing a person 'in the loop'.

## 4. Fluency and factual reliability need to be separated

NIST's Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile identifies confabulation and other content risks as distinctive generative-AI risks and recommends governance, testing, content provenance, documentation and human review calibrated to context (Autio et al., 2024).

The NIST profile is useful because it avoids the trap of treating one fixed hallucination rate as an enduring property of a model. Generative-AI reliability varies by system, task, prompt, information source, deployment configuration and time.

Chapter implication:

> A fluent answer is not sufficient evidence of correctness.

Do not include volatile benchmark percentages unless the chapter later needs one narrow example.

## 5. Human oversight is not automatically effective oversight

The overreliance studies, the jagged-frontier experiment and NIST governance guidance converge on a useful point without proving one universal mechanism:

- human users may be unable to tell when an AI-supported task has crossed outside a reliable capability region;
- people can accept bad advice under some conditions;
- oversight therefore needs task design, verification and governance rather than merely nominal human presence.

This supports the Chapter 9 distinction between human involvement and effective human judgement.

Avoid the claim that 'human-in-the-loop does not work'.

Stronger wording:

> Human oversight only adds value when the human has the information, authority, competence and process needed to challenge the system.

This is author synthesis informed by the evidence and governance literature.

## 6. Expertise does not disappear; its value shifts

The strongest support here comes from heterogeneity rather than a direct universal expertise comparison.

Brynjolfsson et al. show larger productivity gains for less experienced/lower-skilled agents in one customer-support context.

Dell'Acqua et al. show that experienced consultants can still perform worse with AI on an outside-frontier task.

Together, these studies support a nuanced proposition:

- AI can transfer or expose patterns that help less experienced workers perform better in some tasks;
- expertise does not guarantee immunity from AI-induced error;
- expert judgement remains valuable for validation, framing and recognising context that the model may miss.

Do not say experts are always better at detecting AI error. The evidence architecture does not justify that universal claim.

## 7. NIST provides the governance/accountability backbone

The NIST AI RMF and 2024 Generative AI Profile frame risk management as an organisational responsibility across the AI lifecycle. The generative-AI profile notes that GAI may warrant additional human review, tracking, documentation and management oversight, and emphasises governance, pre-deployment testing, content provenance and incident disclosure (Tabassi, 2023; Autio et al., 2024).

This supports the chapter's governance claim that consequential organisational use of AI remains embedded in human and institutional accountability structures.

The line:

> AI can widen the search. It cannot inherit responsibility for the judgement.

is author synthesis.

NIST supports the organisational governance premise underneath it; it should not be cited as if NIST uses that exact formulation.

## 8. Strong claims now safe for drafting

1. Generative AI can materially improve speed and/or quality on some knowledge-work tasks.
2. Performance effects can vary substantially by task and worker.
3. AI assistance can degrade performance on tasks outside a model's effective capability frontier.
4. People can over-rely on AI advice under some experimental conditions.
5. Fluent output should not be treated as sufficient evidence of factual or contextual correctness.
6. Effective oversight requires more than nominal human presence.
7. Current evidence does not justify equating AI-mediated access with durable human expertise.
8. Organisational use of generative AI requires governance, testing, documentation and context-appropriate human oversight.

## Claims still unsafe or unnecessary

Do not claim:

- AI universally benefits novices more than experts;
- experts reliably detect hallucinations;
- generative AI produces a fixed hallucination rate;
- AI makes everyone more creative or more productive;
- human review always catches AI error;
- AI cannot contribute to professional judgement;
- any particular model is reliable or unreliable across all domains;
- AI has 'democratised expertise' in a scientific sense.

## Chapter 9 evidence architecture

### Primary anchor: knowledge-work augmentation

Brynjolfsson, E., Li, D. & Raymond, L.R. 2025, 'Generative AI at Work', The Quarterly Journal of Economics, vol. 140, no. 2, pp. 889-942, doi:10.1093/qje/qjae044.

Use for:
- productivity effects in customer support;
- heterogeneity by experience/skill;
- bounded evidence that AI assistance can support worker learning.

### Primary anchor: jagged frontier

Dell'Acqua, F., McFowland III, E., Mollick, E.R., Lifshitz-Assaf, H., Kellogg, K.C., Rajendran, S., Krayer, L., Candelon, F. & Lakhani, K.R. 2026, 'Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier: Field Experimental Evidence of the Effects of Artificial Intelligence on Knowledge Worker Productivity and Quality', Organization Science, Articles in Advance.

Working-paper version: Harvard Business School Technology & Operations Management Unit Working Paper No. 24-013, first posted 2023 and revised 2026.

Use for:
- task heterogeneity;
- strong gains inside frontier;
- worse correctness outside frontier;
- inability to infer AI value from apparent task similarity.

Final volume/issue/pages/DOI should be verified at manuscript lock if the final bibliographic record has been assigned.

### Primary anchor: overreliance

Klingbeil, A., Grützner, C. & Schreck, P. 2024, 'Trust and reliance on AI — An experimental study on the extent and costs of overreliance on AI', Computers in Human Behavior, vol. 160, article 108352, doi:10.1016/j.chb.2024.108352.

Use for:

- contextual information being overridden;

Pearson, J., Dror, I.E., Jayes, E., Whordley, G.-R., Mason, G. et al. 2026, 'Examining human reliance on artificial intelligence in decision making', Scientific Reports, vol. 16, article 5345, doi:10.1038/s41598-026-34983-y.

## Drafting implication

Chapter 9 should not be organised around AI capabilities versus AI risks.

A stronger structure is:

AI expands reach -> performance benefits are real but jagged -> fluency hides the boundary -> borrowed breadth requires verification -> abundant connections shift scarcity toward judgement -> accountability remains human/organisational.

The evidence therefore supports the chapter's central synthesis:

> **When connections become cheap, judgement becomes expensive.**
