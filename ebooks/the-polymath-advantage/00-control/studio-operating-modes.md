# The Polymath Advantage Studio — Operating Modes

Status: ACTIVE STUDIO CONTROL
Purpose: define the specialist review and development modes used across manuscript development, evidence verification, reconstruction, whole-book review and production.

## Why these modes exist

The Studio uses distinct operating modes so that research, drafting, adversarial review and structural verification are not collapsed into one activity.

Each mode has a different job.

The names are intentionally memorable, but the controls behind them are serious. The mode is selected according to the problem being solved, not for decoration.

## 1. Mole Mode — Exploration and Evidence Discovery

### Mission

Go underground before drafting.

Mole Mode is used when the Studio needs to discover what is known, locate strong evidence, identify intellectual ancestry, test novelty claims and map the boundary between external evidence and author synthesis.

### Typical tasks

- evidence architecture;
- source discovery;
- primary-source preference;
- literature mapping;
- claim-to-source preparation;
- construct ancestry;
- competing explanations;
- evidence gaps;
- chapter exploration before drafting;
- recovery research when source material is missing.

### Operating question

> What is actually known, what is merely plausible, and what would we have to infer ourselves?

### Outputs

- evidence map;
- source register;
- claim-to-source matrix;
- evidence/inference boundaries;
- final drafting brief.

### Rule

**Moles dig before writers build.**

No major chapter should be drafted from enthusiasm alone when the argument depends materially on evidence.

## 2. Wombat Mode — Adversarial Developmental Review

### Mission

Attack the manuscript after it exists.

Wombat Mode is the hostile-reader pass. It assumes that an intelligent sceptic, academic reviewer or experienced professional will challenge the manuscript's strongest claims, favourite phrases and proposed originality.

### Typical tasks

- originality stress test;
- hostile-reader questions;
- overclaim detection;
- alternative explanation testing;
- source/inference boundary challenge;
- repeated concept detection;
- terminology drift;
- chapter-function test;
- claim defensibility;
- unnecessary framework detection;
- sentence-level surgery after structural review.

### Operating question

> If a sceptical expert wanted to break this argument, where would they hit it first?

### Outputs

- adversarial review;
- targeted revision list;
- protect/cut/rewrite decisions;
- developmental gate verdict.

### Rule

**No favourite sentence receives diplomatic immunity.**

Wombat Mode does not rewrite for the pleasure of rewriting. It identifies weaknesses and prescribes the smallest change capable of fixing them.

## 3. Aardvark Mode — Structural and Claim-to-Source Engineering

### Mission

Go deeper than review and test whether the manuscript can carry weight.

Aardvark Mode is the Studio's heavy-engineering pass. It is invoked when a chapter, framework, recovered manuscript, evidence architecture or whole-book section requires foundation-level testing rather than ordinary editorial review.

It combines structural inspection with claim-level verification.

### When to invoke Aardvark Mode

Use it when:

- a chapter has been reconstructed from missing source material;
- a major concept carries book-level importance;
- a source carries several consequential claims;
- a chapter has survived drafting and Wombat review but still needs final structural assurance;
- the manuscript is approaching lock or publication freeze;
- apparently strong prose may be masking weak evidence;
- a whole-book argument needs its load-bearing assumptions tested.

### Core tests

#### Foundation test

Does the chapter's central proposition follow from what came before and support what comes after?

#### Load-bearing claim test

Which claims carry the argument? Are they supported by evidence strong enough for the weight placed on them?

#### Claim-to-source test

For every material factual, empirical, historical, contemporary or contested claim:

- what source supports it?
- does the source actually say that?
- is the studied population/context narrower than the prose implies?
- is the manuscript reporting evidence or making an inference?
- has correlation been converted into causation?
- is a review being treated as primary evidence?
- is an old study being used as though it represents current conditions?

#### Structural continuity test

- Does the chapter duplicate another chapter's job?
- Does the opening create the question the chapter actually answers?
- Does each section change the argument rather than merely add information?
- Does the ending hand the reader naturally to the next chapter?

#### Authenticity test

- Are author-synthesis constructs labelled honestly?
- Is terminology doing explanatory work rather than manufacturing novelty?
- Could the argument survive if the branded terms were removed?

#### Weight test

- Are memorable lines stronger than the evidence beneath them?
- Are examples being mistaken for evidence?
- Is a source carrying more argumentative weight than it can bear?

#### Stability test

- Does the chapter remain coherent if one source is removed?
- Is the argument dependent on one fragile statistic or one fashionable study?
- Are contemporary/AI claims likely to age quickly?

### Operating question

> If we excavate beneath every load-bearing claim, is there enough foundation to keep the structure standing?

### Outputs

- exact body-word count;
- paragraph/section structural audit;
- claim-to-source verification register;
- evidence/inference boundary verdict;
- source fragility list;
- continuity and handoff verdict;
- lock / revise / rebuild decision.

### Aardvark doctrine

> **We dig. We build. We protect.**

The practical Studio interpretation is:

- **Dig:** expose assumptions and trace claims to evidence.
- **Build:** strengthen only the parts that need structural support.
- **Protect:** preserve strong prose, valid synthesis and established chapter boundaries rather than rewriting indiscriminately.

### Rule

**Aardvark Mode is not proofreading.**

If a claim lacks structural support, polishing it does not solve the problem.

The Studio either strengthens the evidence, narrows the claim, marks it explicitly as inference, or removes it.

## 4. Surgical Mode — Targeted Revision

### Mission

Make the smallest necessary change after a problem has been diagnosed.

This is normally activated after Wombat or Aardvark Mode.

### Typical tasks

- narrow an overclaim;
- clarify an evidence boundary;
- remove repetition;
- strengthen a transition;
- insert a missing definition;
- trim rhetorical excess;
- restore target length with substantive argument rather than padding.

### Rule

**Diagnose first. Cut second.**

No wholesale rewrite where targeted surgery will do.

## 5. Lock Mode — Verification Before Manuscript Freeze

### Mission

Confirm that the version being locked is the version that was actually checked.

### Required checks

- exact body-word count from the canonical file;
- references excluded/included according to the house counting rule;
- final claim-to-source verification;
- unresolved Wombat/Aardvark findings closed or consciously accepted;
- title and section structure confirmed;
- no accidental framework or terminology drift introduced during revision;
- canonical file committed to GitHub;
- lock status recorded in control.

### Rule

**Never report a remembered or estimated word count as verified.**

## 6. Whole-Manuscript March

When all chapters exist, the Studio uses the modes sequentially at book level:

1. **Mole** — identify missing evidence, structural gaps and recovery needs.
2. **Wombat** — adversarial whole-book review.
3. **Aardvark** — test the load-bearing architecture and evidence foundations.
4. **Surgical** — perform only targeted whole-book revisions.
5. **Lock** — freeze the canonical manuscript for production.

## 7. Mode-selection guide

Use **Mole Mode** when the problem is: *we do not yet know enough.*

Use **Wombat Mode** when the problem is: *we have an argument and need someone to attack it.*

Use **Aardvark Mode** when the problem is: *we need to know whether the structure and evidence can carry the weight we are placing on them.*

Use **Surgical Mode** when the problem is: *we know exactly what is wrong and need the smallest effective correction.*

Use **Lock Mode** when the problem is: *we believe the work is finished and need proof that the canonical version deserves to be frozen.*

## 8. Studio governing principle

The animal names make the workflow memorable. They do not lower the standard.

Every mode remains subject to the Studio's core controls:

- Australian English;
- plain English for sophisticated ideas;
- verifiable sources;
- evidence/inference separation;
- explicit author-synthesis labelling;
- no invented evidence, cases, statistics, quotations or approvals;
- AI assists; Alec decides.
