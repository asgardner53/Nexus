# Chapter 7

## What Can Actually Be Developed?

There are two easy stories about human potential.

One says talent decides. Some people are simply built for certain forms of achievement. They have the mind, temperament or natural range for it. Others do not.

The other says practice decides. Ability is mostly the result of what people repeatedly do. Work hard enough, train intelligently enough and almost any boundary can be pushed aside.

Both stories are attractive because both make complexity disappear.

Polymathy is especially vulnerable to this argument. If we describe polymathic capability as something people can develop, it is easy to hear a much larger claim: that anyone can become a Leonardo da Vinci with the right habits, reading list and determination. If we push back against that claim, it is equally easy to slide towards the opposite conclusion: that polymaths are simply unusual people with unusual brains, and the rest of us should stay in our lanes.

Neither conclusion follows from the evidence.

The first six chapters have broken polymathic capability into more useful parts. Depth matters. Breadth matters, but breadth alone is not the advantage. Epistemic mobility allows us to change frames. Analogy helps structure travel. Polymathic judgement helps us test what crossed the boundary and what did not.

Now we can finally ask the developmental question properly.

**Before asking whether a capability can be trained, we need to ask what exactly is supposed to change.**

That question changes everything.

There is no well-established psychological trait called polymathic capability that researchers have trained in a controlled study and then measured across the rest of a person's life. There is no credible basis for claiming that a twelve-week intervention can turn a specialist into a polymath. There is also no credible basis for claiming that the capacities involved are fixed at birth.

The more defensible answer sits between those extremes.

**Polymathic capability is developable in parts, not trainable as a single proven trait.**

### The Wrong Question Is Nature or Nurture

Human development rarely respects the clean categories we use to argue about it.

People arrive with different temperaments, cognitive strengths, motivations, histories and opportunities. They then enter environments that amplify some tendencies and suppress others. They acquire knowledge, build skills, form habits, respond to feedback and choose where to invest attention. Those experiences in turn influence what they do next.

This makes the familiar nature-versus-nurture argument too crude for our purpose.

A capability can be partly influenced by stable individual differences and still be developable. A person can begin with an advantage and fail to develop it. Another can begin without the same advantage and still improve substantially. Development does not require everyone to start from the same place or finish in the same place.

Personality research illustrates the point. Joshua Jackson and Amanda Wright describe personality as relatively stable across the lifespan while also reviewing ample evidence that it is malleable. Their account focuses on mechanisms that can contribute to change as well as forces that maintain stability (Jackson & Wright, 2024).

That is a useful antidote to binary thinking. Relative stability does not mean immutability. Malleability does not mean unlimited plasticity.

A 2024 systematic review by Peter Haehner, Amanda Wright and Wiebke Bleidorn reached a similarly nuanced conclusion. Across 30 longitudinal studies of volitional personality change, merely wanting to change was only weakly associated with actual trait change. Interventions designed to support desired change showed positive average effects, but the authors emphasised that the evidence base remains young and that questions about mechanisms, generalisability and practical implications remain open (Haehner, Wright & Bleidorn, 2024).

The openness finding is particularly relevant to this book. Openness to experience is often treated as the obvious personality home of polymathy because it includes receptivity to ideas, imagination and novelty. Yet the review found that evidence for volitional change differed across traits, and associations involving openness were among the weaker or less consistent findings in parts of the literature (Haehner, Wright & Bleidorn, 2024).

So we should resist the temptation to build the theory of polymathic development around one personality dial.

**A disposition can influence where someone starts without determining where they must finish.**

### Curiosity Is Not a Master Trait

Curiosity is an even more seductive explanation.

Polymaths are curious. Curious people explore. Therefore, make people more curious and polymathy follows.

The logic is tidy. Human curiosity is not.

Todd Kashdan and colleagues developed the Five-Dimensional Curiosity Scale partly because existing treatments of curiosity did not capture its full range. Their work distinguished related but separable dimensions, including enjoyment of exploration, sensitivity to information gaps, tolerance for uncertainty or stress, social curiosity and thrill seeking (Kashdan et al., 2018). A later revision separated overt and covert forms of social curiosity while retaining the broader multidimensional account (Kashdan et al., 2020).

The important point here is not the scale itself. It is that a person can be curious in different ways.

One person may love exploring unfamiliar ideas. Another may be driven almost compulsively to resolve a question once they notice a gap in understanding. Another may be highly curious about people while showing little appetite for abstract theory. Another may enjoy novelty only when uncertainty does not become too uncomfortable.

Calling all of them simply curious hides useful differences.

That matters because polymathic development probably does not require one ideal curiosity profile. A technical specialist who dislikes novelty for its own sake may still become highly effective at following difficult questions across disciplinary boundaries. A socially curious leader may build range through conversations rather than solitary reading. A person motivated by unresolved problems may cross fields not because they love breadth, but because the problem refuses to stay inside one field.

This leads to an important piece of synthesis for the book:

**You do not need to become a different personality before you can practise a different pattern of inquiry.**

That is not a claim that repeated inquiry necessarily changes a personality trait. It is a simpler claim about behaviour. Even if broad dispositional tendencies change slowly, people can still change what they repeatedly do: which questions they pursue, whose expertise they seek, which comparisons they make and whether they stop at the first familiar explanation.

This distinction keeps development practical without pretending personality is infinitely editable.

### Practice Matters, But Practice Is Not Everything

If personality is not destiny, perhaps practice is the answer.

The deliberate-practice debate should make us cautious here too.

Brooke Macnamara, David Hambrick and Frederick Oswald conducted a meta-analysis across several performance domains. Deliberate practice explained meaningful proportions of performance differences, but the size of the association varied substantially: it was larger in games, music and sports, and much smaller in education and the professional studies included in their analysis (Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald, 2014).

Those percentages should not be treated as a universal equation telling us how much of expertise is practice and how much is everything else. The domains, measures and study designs differ too much for that.

The more useful conclusion is the authors' broader one: deliberate practice matters, but not as completely as some popular accounts had suggested.

For polymathic development, that is almost exactly the balance we need.

Deep expertise requires sustained learning and practice. Cross-domain fluency requires exposure and use. Recognising patterns across fields becomes easier when those fields are not merely names on a reading list. None of this develops through intention alone.

But practice does not make starting differences disappear. It does not create equal opportunities. It does not guarantee that two people will gain the same amount from the same experience. And it does not establish that practising one component will automatically improve a broad capability somewhere else.

**Practice can move the boundary. It does not make all boundaries disappear.**

This is not a pessimistic claim. It is what makes the developmental promise credible.

### What Can Be Deliberately Improved?

The strongest evidence for the developability argument comes when we stop talking about polymathy as a whole and look at specific components.

Chapter 5 examined one such component: analogical encoding. Jeffrey Loewenstein, Leigh Thompson and Dedre Gentner found that participants who deliberately compared analogous negotiation cases were better able to abstract the underlying principle and use it later. In one experiment, graduate management students who drew an analogy between cases were nearly three times more likely to use the relevant strategy in a later negotiation than participants who studied the same cases separately (Loewenstein, Thompson & Gentner, 1999). Later work likewise found advantages for structured comparison in learning and transfer across negotiation examples (Gentner, Loewenstein & Thompson, 2003).

That matters.

It shows that a process relevant to polymathic thinking can be improved through the way learning is structured. People did not need to acquire a new personality before they could become better at abstracting a transferable relationship.

But the evidence also tells us exactly what not to say.

It does not show that those participants became broadly more polymathic. It does not show that their ability to make distant connections improved across every domain. It does not show that a single intervention changed a general cognitive trait.

It shows improvement in a specific kind of learning and transfer under specific conditions.

That distinction sounds fussy until we realise how much popular training language depends on ignoring it.

A course improves performance on a task and is advertised as improving thinking. A game improves working memory exercises and is advertised as improving intelligence. A workshop changes confidence estimates and is advertised as improving judgement.

Sometimes broader benefits do occur. But they have to be demonstrated rather than assumed.

Improving one mechanism is not the same as demonstrating a general increase in polymathic capability.

### Far Transfer Is the Hard Part

Researchers often distinguish between near transfer and far transfer.

Near transfer means improvement carries to something that still resembles what was practised. Learn one spreadsheet function and you may find a closely related function easier. Practise comparing two negotiation cases and you may become better at recognising the same underlying principle in another negotiation.

Far transfer is the bigger promise. It claims that training in one kind of task improves performance on something substantially different.

This is where many attractive development claims become fragile.

Working-memory training provides a useful cautionary case. Monica Melby-Lervåg, Thomas Redick and Charles Hulme reviewed 87 publications containing 145 experimental comparisons. Their meta-analysis found reliable improvements on trained working-memory tasks and some closely related tasks, but no convincing evidence that the training produced reliable far-transfer improvements in broader abilities such as nonverbal reasoning, verbal ability, reading or arithmetic when appropriate controls were considered (Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme, 2016).

This research is not about polymathy. It should not be used to claim that polymathic development is impossible.

Its value is methodological.

A training effect can be real and still remain close to the training.

That is a sobering principle because the promise of polymathic development is inherently broad. We are interested in whether people can become better at moving among domains, integrating knowledge and judging boundaries. The farther we move from a practised task towards a general capability, the more careful we need to become.

**The farther the promised transfer, the stronger the evidence we should demand.**

That rule protects the reader from hype. It also protects the theory from becoming unfalsifiable.

If every small improvement is counted as evidence for polymathic development, the concept becomes impossible to test. We need to be able to say: this component improved; we do not yet know how far that improvement travels.

### A Training Effect Is Not Yet a Trait

Calibration gives us a modern example of why this distinction matters.

In the previous chapter, we treated calibration in a practical sense: matching the strength of a claim to the quality of the evidence, expertise and uncertainty behind it. It would be convenient if calibration behaved like a general skill that could be upgraded once and then carried everywhere.

The evidence is more awkward.

Megan Kelly and David Mandel examined a commercial calibration course with 70 intelligence analysts. Overall calibration and bias changed after training, but the effect depended on the task. Analysts became better calibrated on interval-estimation tasks, where they had initially been overconfident. On a binary-choice task, however, they were initially underconfident and became even more underconfident after training. Improvement across the two tasks was uncorrelated. Kelly and Mandel concluded that the course appeared to shift confidence downward rather than clearly improve a general metacognitive monitoring ability (Kelly & Mandel, 2024).

Another study by Matthew Martin and David Mandel tested a performance-feedback approach in two much larger experiments and found no improvement in calibration from either the performance-feedback or outcome-feedback training regimes they examined (Martin & Mandel, 2025).

Neither study establishes that calibration cannot improve. Other interventions in other settings may work differently.

What they do show is why we should be wary of converting a task effect into a trait claim.

A person who learns to estimate uncertainty better in one kind of problem has not automatically become well calibrated in negotiations, technical forecasting, hiring decisions and medical judgement.

And underconfidence matters too. If a development programme simply trains people to sound less certain, it can make someone who was already cautious worse calibrated rather than wiser.

**A training effect is not a portable trait until transfer has been demonstrated.**

This principle may be one of the most important in the book because it tells us how to think about development honestly. Improvement is worth pursuing. But we should keep asking where the improvement has actually been shown to travel.

### The Same Opportunity Does Not Produce the Same Outcome

This is easiest to see in an illustrative professional example.

Imagine two experienced specialists are offered the same six-month rotation across strategy, operations and customer experience. Both receive access to the same meetings, the same mentors and the same project brief. At the end of the rotation, their development may still look quite different.

One arrives with deep technical expertise and an instinctive appetite for unfamiliar questions. She uses the rotation to build working models of how commercial, operational and customer decisions interact. She keeps asking what the other functions notice that her home discipline normally ignores. The experience compounds a tendency she already had.

The second specialist is equally capable in his home domain but initially finds the ambiguity uncomfortable. He spends more time trying to translate unfamiliar conversations back into the categories he already knows. Over time, however, repeated exposure gives him enough vocabulary and context to participate more confidently. He may finish the rotation without the same range as his colleague, yet with substantially greater cross-domain capability than he had at the beginning.

A third person might learn less from the same opportunity, not because they lack intelligence or effort, but because the rotation is poorly designed. They attend meetings without meaningful responsibility, receive little feedback and never have to integrate what they encounter into an actual decision. Exposure has occurred. Development has barely been required.

The example is illustrative, but the principle matters. Development is not a vending machine in which identical inputs produce identical capability. Prior knowledge changes what people notice. Motivation changes what they pursue. Practice quality changes what they consolidate. Feedback changes what gets corrected. Opportunity changes what can be attempted.

This is why the phrase *anyone can learn* is simultaneously true and incomplete. People can learn, but they do not learn the same thing from the same experience, at the same rate, or to the same ceiling. A serious theory of polymathic development has to leave room for those differences without turning them into destiny.

Development changes the odds. It does not make people interchangeable.

### Environment Is Part of Development

So far, the chapter could still be read as a story about the individual: their dispositions, their practice and their learning.

That would leave out part of the system.

Robert Tett, Margaret Toich and S. Burak Ozkum's review of Trait Activation Theory describes personality expression as responsive to trait-relevant cues, constraints and facilitators in situations, including task, social and organisational contexts (Tett, Toich & Ozkum, 2021).

In plain English, possessing a tendency does not mean that tendency will appear equally strongly everywhere.

A person who is naturally exploratory may behave very differently in two organisations. In one, they are given autonomy, exposed to different functions and rewarded for asking questions across boundaries. In the other, their role is narrowly prescribed, deviation is punished and cross-functional curiosity is treated as interference.

The person has not become a different human being overnight. The environment has changed what behaviour is invited, useful and safe.

A much earlier study by Kashdan and Mantak Yuen offers a narrower illustration. Among 484 Hong Kong secondary-school students, the relationship between trait curiosity and academic outcomes varied with students' perceptions of the learning environment. Highly curious students did best in settings they perceived as more challenging and growth-supportive, while highly curious students in less challenging perceived environments did worse (Kashdan & Yuen, 2007).

That study does not tell us how curious executives behave in organisations. The participants were adolescents, the context was schooling and the environment measure was specific to that setting.

But it reinforces a wider lesson also present in organisational personality research: what a person brings interacts with what the setting allows and demands.

This matters for polymathic capability because organisations can unintentionally suppress the very behaviours they later say they want.

They ask for innovation but allocate no time for exploration. They praise collaboration but reward narrow functional targets. They want systems thinking but design jobs with no visibility beyond a single process. They celebrate learning but treat every hour not attached to immediate delivery as waste.

In such an environment, a potentially strong boundary-spanner may simply learn to keep their range private.

**Possessing a tendency is not the same as having an environment that allows the tendency to become useful behaviour.**

### Capability Is Not Performance

This suggests a distinction that is useful for the book, provided we do not turn it into another grand framework.

A person can have underlying capacities: aptitudes, dispositions and tendencies that influence what may be possible.

They can develop capabilities: knowledge, skills and practices they are actually able to bring to a problem.

And they can produce performance: what becomes visible in a particular situation, under particular constraints, with particular opportunities.

These are not the same thing.

Consider two experienced technical professionals.

The first is highly curious and quick to see connections but has spent most of their career in a role where performance is defined by narrow technical delivery. They have capacity, but limited opportunity to develop or express broader integrative capability.

The second may be less naturally exploratory, but over years has worked across operations, customers, finance and technology. Repeated exposure has built a repertoire of concepts, questions and relationships that can be brought into new problems.

Put both into a cross-functional transformation and their performance will depend on more than personality. It will reflect what they know, what they have practised, what the task requires and what the environment permits.

This is author synthesis rather than a validated taxonomy, but it prevents an important mistake: judging total potential from one visible performance.

A person may have developed capability that their current role rarely calls upon. Another may perform impressively in a familiar cross-functional setting but struggle when the domains change. A third may need time, trust and repeated exposure before exploratory behaviour becomes visible.

A simpler way to put it is this:

**Capability belongs to the person. Performance emerges from the person in context.**

The sentence is not a causal formula. It is a reminder that development is never only about what is inside the individual.

### What Can We Honestly Promise?

We can now return to the question that started the chapter.

Can polymathic capability be developed?

Yes, but only if we are precise about what the answer means.

We can make strong developmental claims about knowledge and expertise. People can learn. They can deepen a domain, acquire conceptual literacy in another, practise methods, build vocabularies and accumulate experience.

We can make moderately strong claims about some component performances. Structured comparison can improve analogical abstraction and transfer in defined learning settings. Certain training programmes can improve calibration on particular tasks. People can deliberately expose themselves to different domains and become more capable of understanding the questions those domains ask.

We should be more cautious when moving from component performance to broad disposition. Personality traits show both stability and malleability. Curiosity is multidimensional. Some interventions appear able to produce modest desired personality changes, but simply wanting to change is not enough, and effects vary.

We should be more cautious again when claiming far transfer. Improvement near the training task does not guarantee a general upgrade in intelligence, flexibility, judgement or polymathy.

And we should never forget context. The same capability can be expressed differently depending on the opportunities, constraints and incentives surrounding the person.

This gives us an answer that is less dramatic than either destiny or unlimited potential.

It is also more useful.

**The useful question is not whether you can become a polymath. It is whether you can become more polymathically capable.**

For many people, the answer to that narrower question is clearly yes.

You can know more deeply. You can broaden more deliberately. You can improve how you compare examples. You can become more disciplined about what you infer and what you merely suspect. You can create routines and environments that bring exploratory behaviour into use more often.

None of those developments guarantees that everyone will reach the same level. None proves the existence of a single trainable polymathic faculty. None removes the effects of aptitude, motivation, opportunity, time or environment.

Development is meaningful without being limitless.

That may be the most important conclusion of this chapter.

The promise of polymathic development becomes stronger, not weaker, when we refuse to exaggerate it. We no longer need to tell readers that hidden genius is waiting to be unlocked. We can make a more grounded claim: useful parts of the capability can be strengthened, and the evidence tells us enough about the limits to design that development more intelligently.

That changes the next question.

If development is possible but uneven, component-specific and context-dependent, **what should we actually practise?**
