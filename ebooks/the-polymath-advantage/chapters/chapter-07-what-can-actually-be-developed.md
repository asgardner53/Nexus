# Chapter 7

## What Can Actually Be Developed?

There are two easy stories about human potential.

One says talent decides. Some people are simply built for certain forms of achievement. The other says practice decides. Work hard enough, train intelligently enough and almost any boundary can be pushed aside.

Both stories are attractive because both make complexity disappear.

Polymathy is especially vulnerable to this argument. If we describe polymathic capability as developable, it is easy to hear a much larger claim: that anyone can become a Leonardo da Vinci with the right habits and determination. Push back too hard, however, and we can slide towards the opposite conclusion: that polymaths are simply unusual people with unusual minds and the rest of us should stay in our lanes.

Neither conclusion follows from the evidence.

The first six chapters have already broken polymathic capability into more useful parts: depth, breadth, epistemic mobility, structural transfer and judgement. Now we can finally ask the developmental question properly.

**Before asking whether a capability can be trained, we need to ask what exactly is supposed to change.**

There is no well-established psychological trait called polymathic capability that researchers have trained in a controlled study and then tracked across the rest of a person's life. Nor is there good evidence that all the relevant capacities are fixed at birth or immune to development.

The more defensible answer sits between those extremes.

**Polymathic capability is developable in parts, not trainable as a single proven trait.**

### The Wrong Question Is Nature or Nurture

Human development rarely respects the clean categories we use to argue about it.

People arrive with different temperaments, cognitive strengths, motivations, histories and opportunities. They enter environments that amplify some tendencies and suppress others. They acquire knowledge, build skills, form habits, respond to feedback and choose where to invest attention. Those experiences then influence what they do next.

This makes the familiar nature-versus-nurture argument too crude for our purpose.

A capability can be partly influenced by stable individual differences and still be developable. A person can begin with an advantage and fail to develop it. Another can begin without the same advantage and still improve substantially. Development does not require everyone to start from the same place or finish in the same place.

Personality research illustrates the point. Joshua Jackson and Amanda Wright describe personality as relatively stable across the lifespan while also reviewing ample evidence that it is malleable. Their account focuses on mechanisms that can contribute to change as well as forces that maintain stability (Jackson & Wright, 2024).

Relative stability does not mean immutability. Malleability does not mean unlimited plasticity.

A 2024 systematic review by Peter Haehner, Amanda Wright and Wiebke Bleidorn reached a similarly nuanced conclusion. Across 30 longitudinal studies of volitional personality change, merely wanting to change was only weakly associated with actual trait change. Interventions designed to support desired change showed positive average effects, but the authors emphasised that the evidence base remains young and that questions about mechanisms, generalisability and practical implications remain open (Haehner, Wright & Bleidorn, 2024).

Openness to experience is relevant because it includes receptivity to ideas, imagination and novelty. Yet the same review found that evidence for volitional change differed across traits, with openness among the less consistent areas in parts of the literature (Haehner, Wright & Bleidorn, 2024). Its relevance here is adjacent rather than diagnostic: openness may shape exploration, but it is not evidence for a distinct polymath personality type.

**A disposition can influence where someone starts without determining where they must finish.**

### Curiosity Is Not a Master Trait

Curiosity is an even more seductive explanation.

Polymaths are curious. Curious people explore. Therefore, make people more curious and polymathy follows.

The logic is tidy. Human curiosity is not.

Todd Kashdan and colleagues developed the Five-Dimensional Curiosity Scale partly because existing treatments of curiosity did not capture its full range. Their work distinguished related but separable dimensions, including enjoyment of exploration, sensitivity to information gaps, tolerance for uncertainty or stress, social curiosity and thrill seeking (Kashdan et al., 2018). A later revision separated overt and covert forms of social curiosity while retaining the broader multidimensional account (Kashdan et al., 2020).

The important point is not the scale itself. It is that a person can be curious in different ways.

One person may love exploring unfamiliar ideas. Another may be driven to resolve a question once they notice a gap in understanding. Another may be highly curious about people while showing little appetite for abstract theory. Calling all of them simply curious hides useful differences.

For this book, the implication is that there is no reason to assume one ideal curiosity profile is required for polymathic development. A technical specialist who dislikes novelty for its own sake may still become effective at following difficult questions across disciplinary boundaries. A socially curious leader may build range through conversations rather than solitary reading. A problem-driven professional may cross fields because the problem refuses to stay inside one field.

This leads to an important piece of synthesis:

**You do not need to become a different personality before you can practise a different pattern of inquiry.**

That is not a claim that repeated inquiry necessarily changes a personality trait. It is a simpler claim about behaviour. Even if broad dispositions change slowly, people can still change what they repeatedly do: which questions they pursue, whose expertise they seek, which comparisons they make and whether they stop at the first familiar explanation.

### Practice Matters, But Practice Is Not Everything

If personality is not destiny, perhaps practice is the answer.

The deliberate-practice debate should make us cautious here too.

Brooke Macnamara, David Hambrick and Frederick Oswald conducted a meta-analysis across several performance domains. Deliberate practice explained meaningful proportions of performance differences, but the size of the association varied substantially: it was larger in games, music and sports, and much smaller in education and the professional studies included in their analysis (Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald, 2014).

Those percentages should not be treated as a universal equation telling us how much of expertise is practice and how much is everything else. The domains, measures and study designs differ too much for that.

The useful conclusion is simpler: deliberate practice matters, but it is not a complete theory of why people differ.

For polymathic development, that balance matters. Practice can deepen expertise, increase familiarity with other domains and strengthen particular methods. It does not erase starting differences, create equal opportunities or establish that practising one component will automatically improve a broad capability somewhere else.

**Practice can move the boundary. It does not make all boundaries disappear.**

### What Can Be Deliberately Improved?

The strongest evidence for the developability argument comes when we stop talking about polymathy as a whole and look at specific components.

Chapter 5 examined one such component: analogical encoding. Jeffrey Loewenstein, Leigh Thompson and Dedre Gentner found that participants who deliberately compared analogous negotiation cases were better able to abstract the underlying principle and use it later. In one experiment, graduate management students who drew an analogy between cases were nearly three times more likely to use the relevant strategy in a later negotiation than participants who studied the same cases separately (Loewenstein, Thompson & Gentner, 1999). Later work likewise found advantages for structured comparison in learning and transfer across negotiation examples (Gentner, Loewenstein & Thompson, 2003).

That matters because it shows that a process relevant to polymathic thinking can be improved through the way learning is structured.

But the evidence also tells us exactly what not to say. It does not show that participants became broadly more polymathic, that their ability to make distant connections improved across every domain, or that a single intervention changed a general cognitive trait. It shows improvement in a specific kind of learning and transfer under specific conditions.

That distinction sounds fussy until we notice how much training language depends on ignoring it. A course improves performance on a task and is advertised as improving thinking. A game improves working-memory exercises and is advertised as improving intelligence. A workshop changes confidence estimates and is advertised as improving judgement.

Sometimes broader benefits do occur. But they have to be demonstrated rather than assumed.

Improving one mechanism is not the same as demonstrating a general increase in polymathic capability.

### Far Transfer Is the Hard Part

Researchers often distinguish between near transfer and far transfer.

Near transfer means improvement carries to something that still resembles what was practised. Learn one spreadsheet function and a closely related function may become easier. Practise comparing two negotiation cases and you may become better at recognising the same principle in another negotiation.

Far transfer is the bigger promise. It claims that training in one kind of task improves performance on something substantially different.

This is where many attractive development claims become fragile.

Working-memory training provides a useful cautionary case. Monica Melby-Lervåg, Thomas Redick and Charles Hulme reviewed 87 publications containing 145 experimental comparisons. Their meta-analysis found reliable improvements on trained working-memory tasks and some closely related tasks, but no convincing evidence that the training produced reliable far-transfer improvements in broader abilities such as nonverbal reasoning, verbal ability, reading or arithmetic when appropriate controls were considered (Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme, 2016).

This research is not about polymathy. Its value here is methodological: a training effect can be real and still remain close to the training.

That is a sobering principle because the promise of polymathic development is inherently broad. We are interested in whether people can become better at moving among domains, integrating knowledge and judging boundaries. The farther we move from a practised task towards a general capability, the more careful we need to become.

**The farther the promised transfer, the stronger the evidence we should demand.**

That rule protects the reader from hype. It also protects the theory from becoming unfalsifiable. We need to be able to say: this component improved; we do not yet know how far that improvement travels.

There is another implication here. Development is more credible when it is directional rather than generic. A person can deliberately become more knowledgeable in a neighbouring field, better at comparing cases, more disciplined about uncertainty or more experienced at working across functions. Each of those changes has a target that can be described and, at least in principle, observed. The claim becomes weaker when all of them are bundled into a vague promise to become a more flexible or more polymathic thinker.

That distinction matters because broad labels can hide very different developmental pathways. Consider three professionals who all want to become better at cross-domain work. One has excellent technical depth but little exposure to commercial reasoning. Another has broad exposure but tends to make loose analogies without checking them. A third sees multiple perspectives readily but struggles to judge when a specialist should take over. Calling all three 'insufficiently polymathic' tells us almost nothing about what might change. Their developmental problems are different because the missing components are different.

The same logic applies to evidence. If a programme claims to improve cross-domain judgement, we should ask what behaviour or performance would count as improvement. Better retrieval of relevant knowledge? More accurate transfer of structure? Better calibration? Greater ability to recognise a competence boundary? More effective integration of specialist advice? A useful developmental claim should become clearer as we inspect it, not more mysterious.

This does not mean every component can be isolated neatly. Human capabilities interact. Greater domain knowledge can make better analogies possible. Better questions can lead to better exposure. More varied experience can change what someone notices. The point is not to atomise polymathic capability into a hundred independent skills. It is to avoid treating the whole construct as though it were one invisible muscle that simply becomes stronger with exercise.

For the reader, this is encouraging rather than limiting. A general identity can feel inaccessible: either you are the kind of person who spans disciplines or you are not. Components create room for movement. You may not control every starting condition, but you can identify a particular weakness that matters to the work in front of you and improve your capability there. Over time, several such changes may accumulate into something that looks, from the outside, like greater polymathic range.

We should still be careful about the last step. Accumulation is a plausible developmental story, not proof that separate gains automatically combine into a stable general trait. But it gives us a more honest unit of ambition. Instead of trying to manufacture 'polymathy', we can develop the parts of cross-domain thinking that the evidence and the work actually require.

### A Training Effect Is Not Yet a Trait

Calibration gives us a modern example of why this distinction matters.

In the previous chapter, calibration meant matching the strength of a claim to the quality of the evidence, expertise and uncertainty behind it. It would be convenient if calibration behaved like a general skill that could be upgraded once and carried everywhere.

The evidence is more awkward.

Megan Kelly and David Mandel examined a commercial calibration course with 70 intelligence analysts. Overall calibration and bias changed after training, but the effect depended on the task. Analysts became better calibrated on interval-estimation tasks, where they had initially been overconfident. On a binary-choice task, however, they were initially underconfident and became even more underconfident after training. Improvement across the two tasks was uncorrelated. Kelly and Mandel concluded that the course appeared to shift confidence downward rather than clearly improve a general metacognitive monitoring ability (Kelly & Mandel, 2024).

Matthew Martin and David Mandel later tested performance-feedback and outcome-feedback approaches in two larger experiments and found no improvement in calibration from the tested regimes (Martin & Mandel, 2025).

Neither study establishes that calibration cannot improve. They show why we should be wary of converting a task effect into a trait claim. A person who learns to estimate uncertainty better in one kind of problem has not automatically become well calibrated in negotiations, technical forecasting, hiring decisions and medical judgement.

**A training effect is not a portable trait until transfer has been demonstrated.**

### The Same Opportunity Does Not Produce the Same Outcome

Consider an illustrative professional example.

Two experienced specialists are offered the same six-month rotation across strategy, operations and customer experience. Both receive access to the same meetings, mentors and project brief. Their development may still look different.

One arrives with deep technical expertise and an appetite for unfamiliar questions. She uses the rotation to build working models of how commercial, operational and customer decisions interact. She keeps asking what other functions notice that her home discipline normally ignores. The experience compounds a tendency she already had.

The second specialist is equally capable in his home domain but initially finds the ambiguity uncomfortable. He translates unfamiliar conversations back into categories he already knows. Over time, repeated exposure gives him enough vocabulary and context to participate more confidently. If the rotation gives him meaningful responsibility and feedback, he may finish with substantially greater cross-domain capability than he had at the beginning. If it offers only observation without application, the same exposure may produce far less development.

The example is illustrative, but the principle matters. Development is not a vending machine in which identical inputs produce identical capability. Prior knowledge changes what people notice. Motivation changes what they pursue. Practice quality changes what they consolidate. Feedback changes what gets corrected. Opportunity changes what can be attempted.

People can learn without learning the same thing from the same experience, at the same rate, or to the same ceiling. A serious theory of polymathic development has to leave room for those differences without turning them into destiny.

Development changes the odds. It does not make people interchangeable.

### Environment Is Part of Development

So far, the chapter could still be read as a story about the individual: their dispositions, practice and learning. That would leave out part of the system.

Robert Tett, Margaret Toich and S. Burak Ozkum's review of Trait Activation Theory describes personality expression as responsive to trait-relevant cues, constraints and facilitators in situations, including task, social and organisational contexts (Tett, Toich & Ozkum, 2021).

In plain English, possessing a tendency does not mean that tendency will appear equally strongly everywhere.

A person who is naturally exploratory may behave very differently in two organisations. In one, they have autonomy, cross-functional exposure and permission to ask questions across boundaries. In the other, the role is narrowly prescribed and cross-functional curiosity is treated as interference. The person has not become a different human being overnight. The environment has changed what behaviour is invited, useful and safe.

A much earlier study by Kashdan and Mantak Yuen offers a narrower illustration. Among 484 Hong Kong secondary-school students, the relationship between trait curiosity and academic outcomes varied with students' perceptions of the learning environment. Highly curious students did best in settings they perceived as more challenging and growth-supportive, while highly curious students in less challenging perceived environments did worse (Kashdan & Yuen, 2007).

That study does not tell us how curious executives behave in organisations. The participants were adolescents and the context was schooling. But it reinforces a wider lesson also present in organisational personality research: what a person brings interacts with what the setting allows and demands.

Organisations can unintentionally suppress the behaviours they later say they want. They may ask for innovation while allowing no time for exploration, or praise collaboration while rewarding narrow functional targets. In such an environment, a potentially strong boundary-spanner may simply learn to keep their range private.

**Possessing a tendency is not the same as having an environment that allows the tendency to become useful behaviour.**

### Capability Is Not Performance

The same point can be made without turning it into another formal framework.

What a person is disposed towards, what they have learned to do, and what a particular environment allows them to demonstrate are not the same thing.

Someone may be highly curious and quick to see connections but spend years in a role defined by narrow technical delivery. Another person may be less naturally exploratory but, through repeated work across operations, customers, finance and technology, build a repertoire of concepts and questions that can be brought into new problems.

Put either person into a cross-functional transformation and their performance will depend on more than personality. It will reflect what they know, what they have practised, what the task requires and what the environment permits.

This is author synthesis rather than a validated taxonomy, but it protects us from judging total potential from one visible performance.

**Capability may belong to the person, but performance emerges in context.**

### What Can We Honestly Promise?

We can now return to the question that started the chapter.

Can polymathic capability be developed?

Yes, if we are precise about what the answer means.

People can acquire knowledge. They can deepen expertise, build conceptual literacy in adjacent fields, practise methods and accumulate experience. Structured comparison can improve specific forms of abstraction and transfer. Inquiry behaviour can change: people can ask different questions, seek different expertise and deliberately expose themselves to problems that require more than one frame. Where they have influence over the setting, they can also create more opportunities for that behaviour to be used.

Those are meaningful forms of development. They can alter the quality of the questions a person asks, the range of evidence they can recognise and the number of problems they can approach without being trapped inside a single professional vocabulary. None is trivial merely because it falls short of a universal cognitive upgrade.

We should be more cautious when moving from component performance to broad disposition. Personality traits show both stability and malleability. Curiosity is multidimensional. Some interventions appear able to produce desired personality change, but effects vary and merely wanting change is not enough.

We should be more cautious again when claiming far transfer. Improvement near the training task does not guarantee a general upgrade in intelligence, flexibility, judgement or polymathy. Context also matters: the same capability can be expressed differently depending on the opportunities, constraints and incentives surrounding the person.

This gives us an answer that is less dramatic than either destiny or unlimited potential. It is also more useful.

**The useful question is not whether you can become a polymath. It is whether you can become more polymathically capable.**

For many people, the answer to that narrower question is yes.

None of this guarantees that everyone will reach the same level. None proves the existence of a single trainable polymathic faculty. None removes the effects of aptitude, motivation, opportunity, time or environment.

Development is meaningful without being limitless.

The promise of polymathic development becomes stronger, not weaker, when we refuse to exaggerate it. We do not need to tell readers that hidden genius is waiting to be unlocked. We can make a more grounded claim: useful parts of the capability can be strengthened, and the evidence tells us enough about the limits to design that development more intelligently.

That changes the next question.

If development is possible but uneven, component-specific and context-dependent, **what should we actually practise?**
