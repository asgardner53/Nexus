# Chapter 4

## The Ability to Change Lenses

The most dangerous lens is often the one that works.

A useful way of seeing the world rarely announces itself as a lens. It simply feels like reality.

The finance leader sees incentives, trade-offs and return. The engineer sees systems, constraints and failure modes. The lawyer sees obligations, rights and exposure. The psychologist sees behaviour, motivation and cognition. The operations leader sees flow, capacity and bottlenecks.

None of these perspectives is wrong.

In fact, each becomes valuable precisely because it works often enough to earn trust.

That is the problem.

A lens that repeatedly helps us solve problems can stop feeling like a perspective and start feeling like the problem itself. We no longer notice that we are interpreting. We assume we are simply seeing.

Expertise becomes a trap not when it gives us a strong lens, but when it convinces us we are looking at reality without one.

The previous chapter argued that breadth is not the advantage. Breadth enlarges the search space, but depth gives us the substance needed to recognise what matters. Yet even a person with both depth and breadth can remain intellectually stationary.

They may know several disciplines and still approach every problem through the same habitual frame.

A leader may understand psychology but still treat every workforce issue as a motivation problem. An engineer may know a great deal about human factors yet still reach instinctively for a technical fix. A clinician may have studied systems thinking but continue to interpret every failure as an individual performance problem.

The knowledge is present.

The movement is missing.

This chapter is about that movement.

It is about the capacity to notice that the current frame may be inadequate, step outside it long enough to inspect the problem differently and deliberately activate another way of knowing.

I call that capacity **epistemic mobility**.

The phrase is ours for this book. It is not a recognised clinical diagnosis, a validated psychological scale or a settled academic construct. It is a practical synthesis built on established work in cognitive flexibility, expertise, mental set and problem representation.

The idea is simple:

**Polymathic capability depends not only on what you know, but on whether you can change the way you are knowing when the problem demands it.**

### The Lens You Stop Seeing

Every profession trains attention.

That is one of the reasons professional education matters. It teaches people not merely what to know but what to inspect, what to question and what counts as a meaningful distinction.

The accountant learns to see what the novice overlooks in financial statements. The engineer learns to recognise the difference between a surface symptom and an underlying system failure. The clinician learns that two similar complaints can require very different interpretations. The experienced project leader learns that a missed milestone may be a planning issue, a capability issue, a dependency issue or a symptom of something more political.

This ability is enormously efficient.

Without it, every problem would have to be approached as though nothing had ever been learned before.

But efficiency creates habits of representation.

We learn what kind of problem something appears to be, and that representation begins shaping what evidence we notice and what answers feel plausible.

A falling sales number becomes a marketing problem.

Employee turnover becomes an engagement problem.

A project delay becomes a performance problem.

A security incident becomes a technology problem.

These descriptions may be correct.

They may also be premature.

The important intellectual move happens before the solution. It happens at the moment the problem receives a name.

Naming tells us where to look.

If turnover is labelled an engagement problem, we may ask about morale, recognition and manager behaviour. If it is labelled a job-design problem, we may ask about workload, autonomy and role clarity. If it is framed as a labour-market problem, we may ask whether scarce skills simply command better opportunities elsewhere. If it is framed as a strategy problem, we may discover the organisation has built a business model that depends on retaining people in roles no longer attractive enough to retain them.

The facts can remain the same while the questions change.

That is why framing matters.

The first plausible frame can accelerate good judgement. It can also narrow the search before we realise a search has begun.

### When a Good Answer Blocks a Better One

Psychologists have studied a version of this problem for decades under the idea of mental set, sometimes called the Einstellung effect.

The phenomenon is uncomfortable because it does not require a stupid solution.

It can begin with a perfectly good one.

Merim Bilalić, Peter McLeod and Fernand Gobet examined this effect using expert chess players. In one study, chess masters were shown positions containing a familiar solution and, in some cases, a better but less obvious solution. The presence of the familiar move made it harder for players to discover the stronger alternative (Bilalić, McLeod & Gobet, 2008a).

The result matters because the players were not novices trapped by ignorance. They were experts being influenced by the very knowledge that normally made them effective.

A second study went deeper into the mechanism. After expert players found the familiar solution, some reported that they were continuing to search for a better one. Eye-tracking data told a more interesting story. Their attention remained disproportionately drawn towards features of the chess position associated with the solution that had already come to mind (Bilalić, McLeod & Gobet, 2008b).

They were searching.

But not quite freely.

The first activated solution was shaping where they looked next.

This is a powerful image for professional life.

A familiar interpretation can keep attracting attention even after we have consciously decided to consider alternatives.

The finance team says it is exploring the people issue, but keeps returning to incentives.

The technology team says it is exploring the workflow problem, but keeps returning to system features.

The HR team says it is examining the operating model, but keeps returning to capability and behaviour.

The problem is not that experts know too much. It is that what they know can become active before they have decided whether it belongs.

That distinction matters because the lazy conclusion would be to blame expertise.

The research does not justify that.

Bilalić and colleagues' broader chess work explicitly tested competing possibilities: greater expertise might produce rigidity because familiar procedures become highly available, or deeper expertise might support flexibility because experts possess richer knowledge and more alternatives (Bilalić, McLeod & Gobet, 2008a).

Their results were more nuanced than either stereotype. Chess experts could be caught by familiar patterns, but the strongest players were also less susceptible to the effect and more likely to discover the better solution (Bilalić, McLeod & Gobet, 2008a).

Expertise can constrain thought.

Expertise can also help free it.

The issue is not knowledge versus flexibility.

It is whether knowledge remains responsive to the problem in front of us.

### Expertise Is Not the Enemy

This is important for the argument of the book.

There is a fashionable story in which expertise belongs to the old world and flexibility belongs to the new one.

Experts are supposedly trapped by what they know. Outsiders are free because they have fewer assumptions. The future therefore belongs to generalists who can move lightly among fields.

There is enough truth in that story to make it seductive and not enough to make it safe.

Novices have assumptions too. They simply have fewer tested distinctions with which to challenge them.

An outsider can ask a question that insiders have stopped asking. They can also propose an idea that sounds revolutionary because they do not yet know why it failed repeatedly in practice.

Expertise is not the enemy of mobility.

In many situations, it is what makes disciplined mobility possible.

A person who understands one field deeply has at least one reference point for recognising the difference between surface familiarity and serious understanding. They have experienced the moment when a simple explanation became more complicated. They know what it feels like to discover hidden constraints, competing interpretations and exceptions that introductory knowledge did not reveal.

That experience can become another form of rigidity if it turns into “my field is complicated, therefore outsiders cannot contribute”.

But it can also create intellectual humility.

If your own discipline became more complex the longer you studied it, there is a reasonable chance other disciplines contain similar depths you cannot yet see.

That is a useful posture for cross-boundary thinking.

The mature expert, then, is not someone who has somehow eliminated habits of thought. Expertise depends partly on those habits. The more realistic aspiration is to notice when the habit no longer fits and to have enough range to consider another representation.

This is where cognitive flexibility becomes relevant.

### The Capacity to Shift

Cognitive flexibility is an established concept within research on executive functions.

Adele Diamond describes it as one of the core executive functions alongside inhibition and working memory. It includes the ability to change perspectives, adjust approaches to a problem and adapt when demands or priorities change (Diamond, 2013).

The concept is broader than what we need here.

Cognitive flexibility can refer to many forms of switching and adaptation, including shifting tasks, changing rules and considering another person's perspective. It is not a synonym for polymathy and it is not evidence that someone who can switch tasks can automatically integrate multiple disciplines.

But it establishes an important foundation.

Human cognition is not limited to one fixed representation of a situation. We can inhibit an initially dominant response, hold alternatives in mind and reorganise how we approach a problem.

That sounds obvious until we consider how often professional systems reward the opposite.

Organisations create functions because functions build efficiency and accountability. Professional communities create language because language allows precision. Qualifications establish common methods. Teams develop routines because routines reduce cognitive load.

All of this helps work happen.

Then a problem arrives that does not respect the boundaries.

The organisation has developed excellent lenses and weak switching costs.

A challenge moves across technology, behaviour, economics and regulation, but each function naturally activates the interpretation it knows best. Everybody is intelligent. Everybody has relevant evidence. Yet the discussion becomes a competition among frames rather than an investigation of which frame, or combination of frames, best fits the problem.

Cognitive flexibility gives us a useful psychological foundation for understanding why shifting perspectives is possible.

The book needs a more specific concept for what happens when that flexibility is applied to knowledge itself.

### Epistemic Mobility

**Epistemic mobility is the capacity to move deliberately among different ways of framing, questioning and interpreting a problem while remaining aware of the assumptions and limits of each perspective.**

The phrase sounds more academic than the underlying idea.

Epistemic simply relates to knowledge: how we know, what we treat as evidence, what assumptions organise our reasoning and what a particular discipline teaches us to regard as important.

Mobility means movement.

Put them together and epistemic mobility describes a person who can move among ways of knowing without pretending those ways are identical.

That last part matters.

This is not relativism.

A structural engineer's judgement about load-bearing capacity is not merely one interesting perspective among many equally valid viewpoints. A surgeon's clinical expertise cannot be replaced by an enthusiastic workshop on systems thinking. A lawyer's interpretation of legislation carries forms of disciplinary knowledge a manager's intuition does not.

Changing lenses does not flatten competence.

It makes competence easier to position.

A technically precise lens may answer one part of a problem extremely well while leaving another part almost untouched. The question is not whether the technical lens is wrong. It is whether the decision requires more than technical correctness.

Think again about an organisation introducing an automated decision system.

A technical lens may ask whether the model performs accurately enough.

A risk lens may ask what happens when it fails.

A legal lens may ask what obligations surround the data and decision process.

A behavioural lens may ask how people will respond to the recommendation.

An operational lens may ask what happens when the system meets the messy exceptions of actual work.

A leadership lens may ask who remains accountable when the recommendation is machine-generated.

Epistemic mobility is not choosing one of those perspectives because it sounds more sophisticated.

It is recognising that the problem changes depending on the lens, understanding what each lens can legitimately contribute and resisting the urge to let the most familiar one define the whole decision.

A person with epistemic mobility does not ask, “Which discipline is right?”

They are more likely to ask, “What does this discipline make visible, what does it leave out, and what changes if I represent the problem another way?”

That is movement with discipline, not movement for its own sake.

### Change the Question Before You Change the Answer

Consider an illustrative workforce problem.

A business unit has rising absenteeism, falling productivity and growing complaints from managers that employees lack commitment.

The first frame appears quickly.

Motivation.

Managers ask what has happened to engagement. HR considers recognition, performance conversations and manager capability. Leaders discuss accountability. Someone proposes a pulse survey.

Nothing about that response is absurd.

Now change the lens.

Through an operations lens, the same pattern might raise questions about workload, staffing levels, scheduling and process friction. Perhaps employees are not disengaged. Perhaps the system is exhausting them.

Through a job-design lens, the question may become one of autonomy, role clarity and conflicting demands.

Through a technology lens, we might discover employees are working around a new system that has doubled the number of steps required to complete routine tasks.

Through a financial lens, perhaps the organisation has reduced staffing so aggressively that apparent labour productivity has improved on paper while actual work has become fragile.

Through a health and safety lens, some patterns may raise questions about psychosocial hazards and work design that a motivation frame never surfaces.

The point is not that one of these explanations must be correct.

It is that each representation creates a different search.

That changes the questions, the evidence requested and the people whose expertise becomes relevant.

If the original frame had remained invisible, the organisation might have spent months trying to improve employee commitment to a badly designed system.

**Sometimes the biggest intellectual move is not finding a different answer. It is realising you have been answering the wrong kind of question.**

This is one reason epistemic mobility matters so much in leadership.

Senior decisions rarely arrive neatly labelled with the discipline required to solve them.

They arrive as falling performance, rising risk, difficult customers, failed transformations, workforce resistance, technology disruption or strategic uncertainty.

The act of deciding what kind of problem you are facing is already part of the decision.

A leader who frames badly can assemble excellent expertise around the wrong question.

### What Is This Problem, Really?

Making framing conscious does not require a complicated technique.

It begins with noticing the sentence already forming in your head.

“This is a performance problem.”

“This is a technology problem.”

“This is a culture problem.”

“This is a communication problem.”

“This is a capability problem.”

The useful response is not to distrust every first interpretation. That would create paralysis.

It is to hold the interpretation lightly enough to inspect it.

What am I currently treating this as?

What does that frame make visible?

What might it be hiding?

What other discipline or perspective would represent this problem differently?

Those questions are intentionally simple.

Their value comes from interrupting automatic closure.

The first question reveals the lens.

The second respects its usefulness.

The third searches for its blind spot.

The fourth creates movement.

This does not mean running every decision through twelve perspectives. Some problems really are straightforward. If a server has failed because a power supply has physically burned out, a workshop on organisational culture is unlikely to improve the repair.

Epistemic mobility includes knowing when **not** to move.

That is what separates mobility from restlessness.

The aim is not maximum perspective diversity. It is better fit between the representation and the problem.

### A Lens Is Also a Standard of Evidence

Changing lenses is not only about asking different questions. Different fields also teach people to trust different kinds of evidence.

An engineer may prefer measurement, specification and repeatable performance. A behavioural researcher may ask how the data were gathered, what comparison group exists and whether the effect survives alternative explanations. A lawyer may care less about what usually happens and more about what a particular rule, precedent or obligation requires. An experienced operator may place unusual weight on repeated frontline observations because they reveal failure modes that rarely appear in formal reports.

These differences can create frustration. One group thinks another is ignoring hard data. Another thinks the data answer the wrong question. A third believes both are missing the practical reality.

Epistemic mobility does not solve that disagreement by declaring all evidence equivalent. It does something more demanding. It asks us to recognise that the standards by which knowledge is judged are partly shaped by the problem a discipline has learned to solve.

A randomised trial can be extraordinarily powerful evidence for one kind of causal question and almost useless for deciding what a specific organisation should do tomorrow morning when the conditions have never existed before. Frontline experience can reveal an operational problem long before a formal dataset exists, but repeated anecdotes do not automatically establish how common the problem is. A financial model can clarify trade-offs while still depending on assumptions that another discipline needs to challenge.

The ability to change lenses therefore includes changing the question, “What evidence would convince me?”

That is a deeper shift than simply inviting another department to the meeting.

It requires enough humility to recognise that the evidence habits of our home discipline are not universally wrong or universally sufficient. They were built for particular kinds of uncertainty.

This is also where breadth without discipline can become dangerous. A person who moves freely among fields may cherry-pick whichever standard of evidence produces the answer they already prefer. When the numbers are inconvenient, they appeal to lived experience. When experience is inconvenient, they demand controlled evidence. When evidence is uncertain, they retreat to intuition.

That is not mobility. It is opportunism.

Disciplined mobility means allowing another lens to change not only what we see, but what we are prepared to count as a serious test of the explanation.

### Reframing Under Pressure

Changing lenses is hardest when the stakes are high.

Time pressure, accountability and uncertainty all reward familiar interpretations. When a system fails at 2 a.m., nobody wants the incident team conducting a philosophical seminar on alternative frames. When a patient is deteriorating, clinical action cannot wait for every possible perspective to be explored. When a cyberattack is active, speed matters.

This creates an important boundary around epistemic mobility.

The capability is not constant switching. It is the ability to shift at the right moment.

During an immediate response, established expertise and practised routines may deserve priority precisely because they reduce delay. After the immediate threat is controlled, however, the framing can be reopened. Was the incident purely technical? Did incentives encourage unsafe shortcuts? Did procurement create a hidden dependency? Did communication failure delay escalation? Did governance make ownership ambiguous?

In other words, some problems need a narrow lens first and a wider lens later.

The timing of mobility matters.

That observation keeps the concept grounded. We are not arguing that every expert should interrupt skilled action to question the worldview of their profession. We are arguing that capable professionals and leaders need moments when the frame itself becomes available for inspection.

Without those moments, efficient routines can quietly become permanent explanations.

### Mobility Without Drift

There is a risk in celebrating lens-changing too enthusiastically.

Once people discover the pleasure of reframing, every problem starts looking like an opportunity for clever reinterpretation.

The meeting becomes intellectually exciting and practically useless.

A technically sound explanation is discarded because a novel metaphor feels more interesting. A clear legal requirement is treated as merely one perspective. A specialist recommendation is weakened by people who have enough adjacent knowledge to challenge it but not enough depth to understand why it exists.

That is not epistemic mobility.

It is drift.

A new lens has to earn its place.

It should reveal something the existing frame missed, improve the quality of the question, identify a relevant source of evidence or materially change the decision in a way that can be defended.

If it does none of those things, the original lens may be perfectly adequate.

This safeguard returns us to a theme running through the book.

Polymathic thinking is not the accumulation of more perspectives for display.

It is disciplined movement across knowledge boundaries.

Depth matters because it teaches us that fields have internal standards.

Breadth matters because it gives us alternatives.

Epistemic mobility matters because it allows those alternatives to become active when the problem requires them.

But mobility alone still does not create integration.

Changing lenses can show us that another field sees something useful. It does not tell us whether an idea from that field can legitimately be carried into our own.

A biological metaphor may illuminate an organisational problem or distort it.

A concept from physics may clarify a business system or become impressive-sounding nonsense.

A method that works in one discipline may transfer beautifully to another or fail because the underlying structure is different.

Seeing another lens is only the beginning.

The harder intellectual act is carrying structure across the boundary without losing what made it valid in the first place.

For that, we need to understand one of the oldest and most powerful mechanisms of human thought.

Analogy.
