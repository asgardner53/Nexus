# Chapter 2

## The Myth of the Universal Genius

Leonardo da Vinci is probably the worst person with whom to begin a book about developing polymathic capability.

He is also almost impossible to avoid.

More than five centuries after his death, Leonardo remains the cultural shorthand for extraordinary intellectual range. Painter. Draftsman. Anatomical investigator. Observer of plants and water. Designer of machines and weapons. Student of light, mechanics and flight. The surviving record of his work moves across subjects that modern institutions would distribute among different faculties, professions and research centres.

The Metropolitan Museum of Art's major survey of Leonardo's drawings described works spanning landscape, botany, anatomy and military engineering, alongside studies for paintings and designs for machines. It also noted that almost 4,000 sheets of his drawings and notes survive, preserving a remarkable record of his artistic and intellectual investigations (Metropolitan Museum of Art, 2003).

The problem is not Leonardo.

The problem is what we have done with him.

We have turned an extraordinary life into an entrance examination.

When the benchmark for intellectual range is Leonardo da Vinci, most of humanity can be forgiven for specialising.

This matters because the image of the universal genius quietly shapes the way we think about polymathy. The word tends to summon people whose achievements seem impossibly distant from ordinary professional life. Leonardo. Aristotle. Ibn Sina. Benjamin Franklin. Figures remembered not merely for knowing a great deal, but for making substantial contributions across fields that later generations would regard as distinct.

That history is worth taking seriously. It is not, however, a particularly useful development model.

If polymathy means becoming a universal genius, the practical question disappears before we have even asked it. Most people will never produce important work across art, anatomy, engineering and natural philosophy. Most will not become historically significant in even one field. A book claiming otherwise would not be liberating. It would be ridiculous.

But there is another possibility.

What if the useful question is not whether you can become another Leonardo?

What if it is whether some of the capabilities associated with polymathic thinking can be understood, strengthened and applied without pretending that everybody can become an exceptional historical polymath?

That requires us to separate two ideas that are often collapsed into one.

**Polymath is an identity. Polymathic is a capability.**

The distinction sounds small.

It changes the entire book.

### Leonardo Is Real. The Myth Came Later

There is no need to diminish Leonardo in order to make polymathy accessible.

His range really was unusual.

The surviving drawings show sustained investigation rather than a collection of passing curiosities. His anatomical work, for example, included studies based on direct dissection. A Metropolitan Museum catalogue devoted to his anatomical drawings describes fifty sheets made between roughly 1485 and 1510-15 and notes the development of his anatomical and physiological understanding across that period (Keele & Roberts, 1983).

His notebooks also moved through questions of water, gravity, optics, mechanics and celestial phenomena. The Met's survey of the Codex Leicester describes observations ranging from the reflective properties of celestial bodies to gravity and hydrodynamics (Metropolitan Museum of Art, 2003).

That is not dabbling.

Nor was his artistic work somehow separate from this investigative life. Drawing became one of the means through which Leonardo explored problems. The same hand that studied composition and human expression also examined skulls, plants, water and mechanisms. On one sheet, artistic and mechanical thinking could literally occupy opposite sides of the page.

It is tempting to turn such a life into a romantic story about limitless genius.

That temptation should be resisted.

Historical achievement reflects more than individual ability. Opportunity, patronage, education, institutions, technology, social position and the structure of knowledge all shape what becomes possible. History also remembers unusual people selectively. The people who become symbols are rarely representative samples.

Leonardo therefore gives us evidence that extraordinary intellectual range can exist. His biography does not tell us how much of that range can be reproduced by another person living in another century under radically different conditions.

History can show us what happened.

It cannot, by itself, tell us what is trainable.

This is an important discipline for the argument ahead. We will encounter remarkable people throughout this book. Their lives can reveal patterns, possibilities and tensions. They can help us ask better questions. They cannot carry empirical claims they were never designed to answer.

Leonardo is a case.

He is not a controlled experiment.

Once that distinction is clear, we can admire him without allowing him to dominate the definition.

### How Genius Became the Definition

The universal-genius image creates a binary.

On one side stands the polymath: unusually intelligent, endlessly curious, accomplished across numerous fields and perhaps historically significant.

On the other side stands everybody else.

This framing produces several assumptions that feel plausible because the famous examples are so extreme.

A polymath must know an enormous amount. A polymath must achieve mastery in several fields. A polymath must possess unusual natural intelligence. Polymathy belonged to an earlier period when there was less knowledge to master. Modern professionals, living in an age of extreme specialisation, therefore have little realistic reason to think about it.

There are pieces of truth inside some of these intuitions.

Exceptional achievement across multiple domains is rare. Developing serious proficiency in one difficult field can require years of learning and practice. Doing so across several fields creates obvious constraints of time, opportunity and attention.

Research on creative polymathy makes precisely this point while also complicating the binary. James Kaufman, Ronald Beghetto, John Baer and Zorana Ivcevic argue that creative polymathy at the highest levels is rare in part because becoming proficient in any field normally requires substantial training. Yet they also argue that when creativity is considered at multiple levels rather than only through historically eminent achievement, creative polymathy may be considerably more common than the famous cases suggest (Kaufman et al., 2010).

That is a useful correction, but it needs careful handling.

Creative polymathy is not identical to the broader idea of polymathic capability developed in this book. Nor does evidence that people can create across domains mean everybody can become deeply expert in whatever they choose.

The more useful implication is that our definition changes what becomes visible.

If we define polymathy only by the rarest historical achievements, we will find rare historical achievers.

If we ask instead whether people differ in the extent to which they develop breadth, depth and connections among areas of knowledge, we have moved from a hall of fame to a developmental question.

That does not lower the standard.

It changes the object of study.

We do this elsewhere without difficulty. A person can develop leadership capability without becoming Nelson Mandela. A person can improve mathematical reasoning without becoming Terence Tao. A person can become a stronger writer without becoming Toni Morrison.

The existence of exceptional performers does not make a capability meaningless for everybody else.

Yet with polymathy, we have often allowed the exceptional example to swallow the category.

The result is strangely self-defeating. The more inspiring the historical figure becomes, the less useful the idea becomes to the reader.

The solution is not to declare everybody a polymath.

That would create the opposite problem.

### Curiosity Is Not Enough

There is a wonderfully flattering version of polymathy circulating in popular culture.

You like history and technology. You read psychology books. You have several hobbies. You listen to science podcasts while cooking.

Congratulations. You are a polymath.

No.

You may be curious, and curiosity is valuable. You may have broad interests, which can create fertile conditions for learning. You may even be beginning to build something important.

But a crowded bookshelf is evidence of a crowded bookshelf. What matters is what the knowledge becomes capable of doing together.

If we make polymathy synonymous with curiosity, the concept becomes pleasant and almost useless.

Curiosity is the desire to know, explore or understand.

Breadth concerns meaningful engagement across different areas of knowledge or practice.

Depth concerns the substance of that engagement. It asks whether the person has moved beyond surface familiarity towards a level of understanding or capability that can withstand serious use.

Integration concerns what happens between those areas. Do they remain separate possessions, or does knowledge from one domain alter how the person understands, questions or acts in another?

These distinctions matter because the combinations produce very different intellectual profiles.

Imagine someone who reads widely across economics, neuroscience, history, design, artificial intelligence and philosophy. They can discuss each enthusiastically. They recognise the famous thinkers. They have absorbed the vocabulary. Yet their understanding remains largely introductory.

That is breadth without much depth.

It may be an excellent beginning.

It is not yet a strong case for polymathy.

Now imagine someone with substantial competence in engineering, psychology and design. They have worked seriously in each area, but the fields remain compartmentalised. Engineering is used for engineering problems. Psychology sits in another mental drawer. Design appears when design is requested.

That person possesses impressive range and depth.

But something is still missing.

The knowledge has accumulated without necessarily interacting.

This is where contemporary research becomes particularly useful.

### Breadth, Depth and Integration

Polymathy is not a mature research field with one universally accepted definition.

That sentence matters.

The concept has been approached through biographies of eminent individuals, studies of creativity, education, careers, psychology and other neighbouring literatures. Researchers have not always meant exactly the same thing when they use the term.

One of the most useful recent attempts to bring greater precision to the concept comes from Michael Araki's doctoral research at the University of Louisville. Araki frames polymathy as an expansive approach to pursuing, developing and applying knowledge characterised by the conjunction of three dimensions: breadth, depth and integration (Araki, 2025).

His dissertation also develops and validates a Polymathic Orientation Scale designed to examine the extent to which people value, prefer and habitually pursue those dimensions (Araki, 2025).

This work is highly relevant to our argument.

It should not be turned into more than it is.

Araki's dissertation is a recent contribution to an emerging area of scholarship. It does not establish that psychology has reached a final definition of polymathy, nor does one doctoral programme settle all the developmental questions we will examine later.

But the breadth-depth-integration formulation gives us a powerful way to escape both traps: the impossible universal genius and the cheerful dilettante.

Breadth matters because polymathic thinking crosses boundaries.

Depth matters because superficial exposure does not provide enough substance to make those crossings reliable.

Integration matters because separate stores of knowledge do not automatically produce new understanding.

The three dimensions also restrain one another.

Breadth without depth can become intellectual tourism.

Depth without breadth can remain highly specialised expertise.

Breadth and depth without integration can become a portfolio of disconnected competencies.

Integration is what makes the relationship interesting.

It also creates only a possibility, not a guarantee of correctness. Different fields can be connected badly. A borrowed model can be inappropriate. A compelling synthesis can still be wrong. Later chapters will examine how connections should be tested rather than merely admired.

This is one reason the specialist-versus-generalist debate is too crude for the problem we are examining. It treats breadth and depth as if they sit at opposite ends of a seesaw: more of one must mean less of the other.

Real intellectual development is messier.

A person can be deeply expert in one area and moderately capable in several others. Another can develop two substantial domains over a career. Another may possess one professional depth while deliberately building enough literacy elsewhere to recognise patterns, interrogate assumptions and collaborate intelligently.

The profile can change over time.

What matters is not whether every domain reaches identical depth.

What matters is whether there is enough substance for knowledge to interact responsibly.

Breadth, depth and integration are not original to this book. The organising move I am making is different: to shift attention from the status question, **Who counts as a polymath?**, towards the developmental question, **What would it mean to become more polymathic?**

That is a narrower claim.

It is also a more useful one.

### When Knowledge Meets Knowledge

Consider three professionals confronted with the design of a new automated safety system in a large industrial operation.

The first is a highly experienced engineer. She understands the machinery, failure modes, operating tolerances and technical requirements in great depth. Her expertise is indispensable.

The second has read widely about engineering, behavioural science, user experience, organisational culture, artificial intelligence and safety. He can generate an impressive range of ideas, but his knowledge of each field is shallow enough that he sometimes misses distinctions practitioners would regard as fundamental.

The third began as an engineer and retains substantial technical depth. Over time, she has developed meaningful knowledge of human factors and design because technically correct systems still have to be used by human beings.

When she examines the proposed safety system, she does not merely ask whether the technology can detect a hazard. She also asks what operators will do when the system produces repeated warnings, whether the interface communicates uncertainty clearly and whether a technically safer system could create new workarounds.

The engineering questions change because another body of knowledge has entered the conversation.

This is an illustrative scenario, not evidence that multidisciplinary individuals always make better safety decisions.

Its purpose is narrower.

It shows what integration looks like.

The interesting question is not how many fields exist in someone's head. It is whether those fields ever meet.

Knowledge from one domain changes the questions asked in another.

That is different from simply having several interests.

It is also different from pretending to be an expert in everything.

The engineer in our example does not need to become a psychologist or professional interface designer. In a serious project, she may still need both. Her wider knowledge does something else: it helps her recognise relevant dimensions of the problem, communicate across boundaries and identify when another form of expertise is needed.

Later, we will examine how digital search and artificial intelligence complicate the difference between access and understanding. For now, the point is simpler.

Polymathic capability cannot mean frictionless access to many subjects.

It has to involve something more substantial.

### Polymath Versus Polymathic

We can now make the distinction more precise.

A **polymath** is usually a descriptive label applied to a person judged to possess substantial knowledge, capability or achievement across multiple domains.

The label carries historical and cultural baggage. It invites arguments about thresholds. How many fields count? How deep must the knowledge be? Must the person create original work? Does professional competence count? Does recognition by others matter? Can somebody be a polymath without public achievement?

Those are legitimate scholarly questions.

They are not the questions this book most needs to answer.

**Polymathic capability** is our practical organising concept.

By it, I mean the developing capacity to build meaningful knowledge across domains and integrate those domains in ways that improve understanding or action.

This is a synthesis for the purposes of this book.

I am not claiming to have discovered a new psychological construct, nor that this wording is an established academic definition. It draws on contemporary polymathy research, particularly the importance of breadth, depth and integration, and the rest of the book will test what additional mechanisms are needed to make that capability useful and responsible.

That transparency matters because attractive ideas can easily outrun their evidence.

If we call polymathic capability a capability, we immediately create an empirical obligation.

People differ in interests, experiences, opportunities and learning histories. Those differences matter, but this chapter does not need to settle how much each contributes.

The developmental argument therefore cannot be that everyone is secretly Leonardo.

Nor can it be that anybody can master anything if they try hard enough.

Araki's work treats polymathic orientation as something that varies across individuals rather than a binary status (Araki, 2025). That does not yet prove how far the underlying behaviours can be strengthened, but it makes the developmental question legitimate.

How far can it be strengthened?

Under what conditions?

With what constraints?

Those are questions the book must earn the right to answer.

For now, the shift from noun to adjective gives us somewhere useful to begin.

You do not need to declare yourself **a polymath**.

You can ask whether your thinking is becoming **more polymathic**.

### A Capability, Not a Club

Identity labels are seductive.

They offer belonging.

They also invite performance.

The moment polymath becomes an identity to defend, a curious thing can happen. Learning stops being the point. The label becomes the point.

People begin collecting fields as credentials of intellectual range. Every interest becomes evidence. Every career change becomes proof. Breadth becomes biography rather than capability.

That is not where the advantage lies.

The useful version of polymathy is less glamorous.

It involves discovering that another field has something to teach you and then doing enough work to understand what that something actually means.

It involves tolerating the return to novice status after years of professional competence.

It involves learning new vocabulary without mistaking vocabulary for understanding.

It involves noticing when a concept from one domain illuminates another and when the analogy is superficial.

It involves carrying expertise across a boundary without assuming the rules remain unchanged on the other side.

And it involves knowing when to stop pretending you understand and ask somebody who does.

There is humility inside serious polymathic thinking.

That may sound strange in a chapter that began with universal genius.

But the more domains a person encounters seriously, the more opportunities they have to discover the scale of what they do not know.

Breadth can inflate confidence when it is shallow.

Breadth can also discipline confidence when it repeatedly exposes the limits of one's own models.

This is one reason we should be cautious about treating polymathy simply as an accumulation problem.

The goal is not to cram more subjects into a finite mind.

The goal is to change the quality of the relationships among what we know, what others know and what a problem requires.

That shift also makes polymathic capability relevant to people who will never change professions.

A surgeon may remain a surgeon while developing a richer understanding of systems design, communication or behavioural science that changes how she approaches patient safety.

A software engineer may remain deeply technical while learning enough about organisational behaviour and economics to recognise why technically elegant products fail inside real institutions.

A senior HR professional may develop sufficient literacy in data, technology, finance and work design to ask better questions without pretending to replace the specialists in those fields.

A leader may never become the deepest expert in any of the functions reporting to them, yet become unusually capable of seeing relationships among those functions.

The common feature is not career variety.

It is knowledge becoming connected enough to alter judgement.

This brings us back to the problem established in Chapter 1.

The modern world does not suffer from a simple shortage of specialists.

It often suffers from difficulty connecting specialised knowledge when problems cross the boundaries we have created.

The answer is not a workforce of universal geniuses.

It is a stronger capacity, in individuals and eventually in teams, to move among areas of knowledge without abandoning the disciplines that make knowledge trustworthy.

That is a much less romantic proposition than becoming Leonardo.

It is also far more useful.

### Becoming More Polymathic

So, can this ability be developed?

We are not ready to answer that with a triumphant yes.

That restraint is deliberate.

A serious developmental claim has to distinguish among curiosity, personality, expertise, learning strategies, transfer, opportunity and practice. Those questions belong later, where the evidence can be examined properly.

For now, one point is enough.

Development does not have to end at genius to matter.

If a technical specialist becomes better at recognising the human dimensions of a technical problem, that matters.

If a leader becomes better at recognising when an unfamiliar field contains knowledge relevant to a decision, that matters.

If a scientist becomes better at identifying a useful method from another discipline, that matters.

If a professional becomes better at moving from surface exposure towards genuine working understanding, that matters.

None of these achievements requires a statue.

They require movement.

This is why the adjective is so powerful.

**More polymathic** allows development without pretending there is a simple finishing line.

It allows us to examine degrees, mechanisms and trade-offs.

Most importantly, it gives us permission to investigate the proposition rather than preach it.

You may never know whether history would call you a polymath.

That is not the useful test.

The useful question is whether the range, depth and integration of your thinking can become stronger than they are today.

The rest of this book is an attempt to find out what that would require.

But before we can explore how knowledge travels, we have to deal with another seductive simplification.

Breadth is easy to celebrate.

It sounds open, curious and modern. It is the part of polymathy most visible in biographies and easiest to imitate. Read widely. Explore more. Cross boundaries. Learn something new.

All good advice.

None of it guarantees an advantage.

Breadth without depth can become dabbling. Depth without breadth can become confinement. Even possessing both does not guarantee they will interact.

The debate between specialist and generalist therefore misses the more interesting question.

**What happens when depth learns to travel?**

That is where we go next.
