# Chapter 6

## Where the Analogy Breaks

The most dangerous moment in cross-domain thinking may be the moment the analogy works.

There is a small rush when a difficult problem suddenly becomes intelligible. The pieces line up. A pattern from somewhere else appears to fit. What was complicated begins to feel familiar.

That feeling is useful. It is also where judgement can fail.

The previous chapter showed how structure can travel across domains. But every useful transfer leaves something behind. The point at which an analogy becomes persuasive is therefore not the point at which our work is finished. It is the point at which another question becomes necessary:

**What did not transfer?**

That question matters because cross-domain thinkers face a particular kind of risk. Breadth can make unfamiliar territory feel navigable before it is truly understood. Vocabulary becomes familiar. Patterns become recognisable. A few important ideas begin to connect. Confidence rises, and the boundary between relevance and competence starts to blur.

This chapter is about preserving that boundary without turning it into a wall.

**Connection is only half the skill. The other half is calibration.**

### When the Analogy Feels Too Good

Return for a moment to the hospital-flow example from the previous chapter.

The traffic analogy can reveal useful structure. Variable arrivals meet constrained capacity. Queues form. A blockage downstream creates congestion upstream. Priority rules alter who moves first. Those relationships are real enough to make the analogy productive.

Then the analogy starts to feel natural. Managers begin talking about patient throughput. Beds become capacity units. Discharge becomes flow release. Waiting becomes queue time. The vocabulary helps because it allows complex operations to be discussed with precision.

But precision of language can create a false sense that the model is complete.

Patients are not vehicles. Clinical urgency differs. Treatment duration is not simply processing time. The morally relevant cost of waiting is not the same for every person. A patient may need to stay in a bed precisely because moving them faster would be unsafe. A queue can represent inefficiency, but it can also reflect necessary clinical prioritisation.

The analogy is still useful. It has simply reached a boundary, and that boundary is part of understanding the analogy properly.

A disciplined transfer therefore has two sides. We need to know what corresponds between the source and target, and we need to know which important properties remain specific to the target.

A useful transfer explains both the correspondence and the break.

This sounds obvious once stated. In practice, the break is easy to ignore because recognition feels so satisfying. Human beings are not especially reliable judges of how deeply they understand something merely because it feels familiar.

### The Feeling of Understanding

Leonid Rozenblit and Frank Keil demonstrated a striking version of this problem in their research on what they called the illusion of explanatory depth.

Participants were asked to rate how well they understood the workings of familiar mechanisms and systems. They then had to explain those mechanisms in detail and later reassess their understanding. Across the studies, people often discovered that their initial sense of explanatory knowledge exceeded what they could actually produce when required to describe how the system worked (Rozenblit & Keil, 2002).

The effect was not equally strong for every kind of knowledge. Rozenblit and Keil found it especially pronounced for explanatory knowledge about systems with causal mechanisms, compared with several other forms of knowledge such as facts or narratives (Rozenblit & Keil, 2002).

The lesson is not that people know nothing about familiar objects or that self-assessment is always hopelessly inaccurate. It is narrower and more useful: we can feel we possess a coherent causal explanation before our actual explanatory model is deep enough to justify that feeling.

Cross-domain learning may create a similar risk. A professional enters a neighbouring field. They learn the central vocabulary. They understand the headline models. They can follow a discussion. They recognise the major questions. They may even become good at explaining the field to other non-specialists.

That is real learning, but it is not the same as deep competence. The danger is what happens in the middle, when fluency grows faster than judgement.

A leader can learn enough about machine learning to discuss bias, training data and model performance without being able to evaluate a technical model independently. An engineer can learn enough behavioural science to recognise incentive problems without being qualified to design or validate a psychological intervention. A strategist can learn enough ecology to use concepts such as resilience and adaptation while still misunderstanding where biological mechanisms cease to map onto organisations.

None of those people should stop learning. The point is that learning creates new forms of responsibility as well as new possibilities.

**Familiarity can feel like understanding long before understanding has arrived.**

That is why a polymathic thinker needs more than the ability to connect. They need some way to judge the strength of the knowledge behind the connection.

### Confidence Is Not Calibration

Confidence is often treated as though it were a personality variable. Some people are confident. Others are cautious. Leaders are told to project confidence. Experts are expected to sound certain. Humility is sometimes confused with softening every conclusion until nothing definite remains.

None of that is what calibration means.

Stephen Fleming's review of metacognition and confidence describes confidence judgements as inferences about the quality or correctness of our own decisions. Those judgements can be useful, but they are not identical to task performance. People form beliefs about their own cognitive processes using incomplete and sometimes imperfect information, which means confidence and accuracy can diverge (Fleming, 2024).

I use calibration here in a practical sense: adjusting the strength of a claim to the quality of the evidence, expertise and uncertainty behind it.

**The goal is not lower confidence. It is better-matched confidence.**

If the evidence is strong, the reasoning has been tested and the domain falls inside your competence, high confidence may be entirely appropriate. If the evidence is weak, the analogy is untested and the judgement depends on knowledge you only partly understand, confidence should fall.

Calibration asks whether the strength of the conclusion matches the strength of the basis for that conclusion.

This matters because underconfidence can be costly too. A capable professional who habitually distrusts their own well-founded judgement may defer unnecessarily, delay decisions or surrender useful insight to louder voices. Intellectual humility should not train people to become tentative about everything.

The calibrated professional is not the one who says “I could be wrong” after every sentence. It is the one who can say why they are confident, what the confidence rests on, what remains uncertain and what evidence would cause them to revise the conclusion.

That creates a practical question that cross-domain thinkers cannot avoid: where does my competence actually end?

### Where Does Your Competence End?

The answer is not always obvious because competence is not binary.

You can know enough about a field to recognise that it matters without knowing enough to practise inside it independently. You can know enough to ask a specialist a good question without knowing enough to evaluate every technical detail of the answer. You can understand the implications of an expert judgement for a wider decision without possessing the expertise needed to generate that judgement yourself.

These are different forms of participation.

Imagine a senior executive evaluating the introduction of an automated decision system. She understands the business purpose, the workflow and the strategic trade-offs. She has developed enough literacy in AI to ask what data the model uses, how performance is measured and what happens when confidence is low. She also understands enough governance to ask who remains accountable for the final decision.

That breadth is valuable. But it does not automatically qualify her to audit the model architecture, interpret every statistical validation result or give a definitive legal opinion on the system's compliance obligations.

The boundary is not a failure of the executive's breadth. It is evidence that the breadth is being used honestly.

**The boundary is not a fence. It changes what you can responsibly claim.**

That distinction helps us escape the tired instruction to “stay in your lane”. Cross-domain problems rarely stay inside lanes. Leaders, designers, researchers and technical professionals often have to reason across areas where they are not the deepest expert in the room. Refusing to cross those boundaries would make integration impossible.

But crossing a boundary does not erase the difference between insight and authority.

You may responsibly say, “This behavioural pattern appears relevant to the technical design,” without being able to independently validate the behavioural model. You may recognise that legal risk changes a decision without being able to settle a complex interpretation of legislation yourself. You may challenge an expert's assumptions without assuming your challenge automatically outranks their depth.

Knowing that expertise is needed is valuable. It is not the same as possessing the expertise you need.

This is where intellectual humility becomes useful, provided we do not turn it into intellectual helplessness.

### Humility Without Helplessness

Intellectual humility has become a popular virtue, sometimes so popular that it risks becoming vague.

Tenelle Porter and colleagues, reviewing research across psychology and related fields, identify a common thread in the concept: awareness of limitations in one's knowledge and beliefs, including recognition that those beliefs may be wrong (Porter et al., 2022).

That is useful for our purposes because it separates humility from personality performance. An intellectually humble person does not have to speak softly, avoid conclusions or treat every view as equally plausible. They need to remain aware of the limits of the basis on which they are speaking.

Humility is not thinking less of what you know. It is knowing where what you know stops.

That is especially important in polymathic work because broad learning can generate legitimate confidence in one moment and unjustified confidence in the next. A person may move through several domains in the same discussion. In one, they possess deep expertise. In another, they have working knowledge. In a third, they are merely able to recognise that the issue exists.

If the tone of certainty remains unchanged across all three, the listener cannot see the competence boundary and the thinker may stop seeing it too.

Calibrated participation therefore requires something surprisingly difficult: allowing the strength of your claim to change as the depth of your knowledge changes. That does not weaken your contribution. It often makes the contribution more trustworthy.

A technical leader who says, “I am confident about the architecture, but I want a human-factors specialist to test my assumptions about operator behaviour” is not retreating from leadership. They are locating uncertainty accurately. A CEO who says, “I understand the commercial implications, but I need legal advice before I treat this interpretation as settled” is not surrendering judgement. They are separating the wider decision from one specialist component of it.

Intellectual humility concerns recognising limits in what we know. Polymathic judgement goes further: it concerns what we do when reasoning across those limits.

That leads to another difficulty. What happens when the experts themselves disagree?

### Expertise Deserves Weight, Not Worship

“Ask an expert” sounds like a clean rule until two experts give different answers.

Real decisions frequently cross fields in which evidence is incomplete, assumptions differ and professional judgement plays a legitimate role. Two credible specialists can look at the same problem and disagree about the model, the interpretation, the level of risk or the appropriate action.

Intellectual humility does not resolve that disagreement by telling us to surrender to whichever expert speaks first.

Duncan Pritchard, writing philosophically about intellectual humility and peer disagreement, argues against the idea that humility automatically requires a conciliatory reduction in confidence simply because an epistemic peer disagrees (Pritchard, 2021). That is a philosophical argument rather than a behavioural decision rule, but it gives us a useful warning: humility should not be confused with automatic capitulation.

In professional life, expert disagreement changes the task. Instead of asking, “Which expert should I believe?”, we often need to ask what the disagreement is actually about.

Are they answering the same question? Are they working from different assumptions or evidence? Does one specialist own one part of the problem while the other is speaking to another? Are they disagreeing over facts, models, values, thresholds of acceptable risk or the consequences of being wrong?

Those distinctions matter.

An engineer and a lawyer may appear to disagree about whether a system is “safe”, when one is speaking about technical failure probability and the other about legal exposure. Two clinicians may accept the same evidence but disagree about the acceptable balance between benefit and harm. Two economists may use different assumptions about future behaviour rather than different historical facts.

The presence of expertise does not remove the need to frame the decision well.

**Expertise deserves weight. It does not eliminate judgement.**

That sentence should not be used as permission to ignore specialists whenever their conclusion is inconvenient. Quite the opposite. Expertise deserves weight because depth matters.

Nor does integration always mean that the broad decision-maker should resolve a disputed technical question. Sometimes the responsible conclusion is that credible expert disagreement remains unresolved and the wider decision must reflect that uncertainty. Good judgement can include carrying uncertainty forward rather than manufacturing a certainty the evidence cannot support.

The point is that an integrated decision may still require someone to understand how several forms of specialist judgement fit together, especially when no single expert owns the whole problem.

Which brings us to the handoff.

### The Calibrated Handoff

Part of mature knowledge is knowing where knowledge lives.

Research on transactive memory systems offers a useful neighbouring idea. Rather than requiring every member of a group to know everything, groups can develop shared awareness of who possesses specialised knowledge. Peltokorpi and Hood's review describes how members of dyads and teams learn “who knows what”, specialise in different information domains and retrieve information from relevant domain experts (Peltokorpi & Hood, 2019). Argote and Ren similarly describe transactive memory as a collective system for encoding, storing and retrieving knowledge, including a kind of directory of where specialised knowledge resides (Argote & Ren, 2012).

Transactive-memory research concerns collective knowledge systems. I draw a narrower individual implication from it here: capable boundary-spanners benefit from knowing where relevant depth resides.

Part of knowing more is knowing who knows more than you.

That sounds almost trivial until you watch it fail.

A leader knows a decision has legal implications but asks the wrong lawyer. A project team knows human factors matter but seeks a generic training solution instead of expertise in work design. A technical group recognises a security issue but escalates it to someone whose expertise is infrastructure rather than threat modelling. The category “expert” is too broad to be useful if the expertise does not match the question.

A good handoff therefore involves more than admitting a limit.

First, the boundary has to be recognised. The thinker needs enough breadth to notice that the problem has entered another knowledge domain. Then the relevant depth has to be selected. Not everyone with credentials in a broad field possesses the same expertise. The fit between specialist and question matters. Finally, the question has to be translated. The specialist needs enough context to understand the wider decision, and the person making the wider decision needs enough understanding to integrate the specialist answer back into the whole.

Consider again the executive evaluating an automated decision system.

The data scientist may answer questions about model performance and validation. The privacy specialist may identify constraints on data use. The lawyer may interpret legal obligations. The operations lead may explain how exceptions actually occur in practice. The HR or workforce specialist may identify effects on roles, fairness and accountability.

None of those answers automatically becomes the decision. Someone still has to understand their interaction.

A technically acceptable model may create an unacceptable workflow. A legally permissible process may create a trust problem. A commercially attractive automation may produce a governance gap. A workforce safeguard may reduce the efficiency benefit that justified the investment in the first place.

The handoff therefore cannot become a way of handing away responsibility.

**Handoff is not abdication. Someone still has to reconnect the specialist answer to the whole problem.**

That reconnection is one of the places where polymathic judgement becomes visible.

### Good Boundaries Still Matter

The language of boundary spanning can make boundaries sound like obstacles. Sometimes they are. Silos can prevent useful knowledge from moving, professional jargon can exclude outsiders, and organisational structures can make collaboration unnecessarily difficult.

But boundaries also perform useful work. They mark competence, preserve methods and establish accountability. They make it possible to know who is responsible for a judgement and what standards were used to produce it.

Samer Faraj and Aimin Yan's study of knowledge teams examined several forms of boundary activity, including boundary spanning, buffering and reinforcement. In their study of software development teams, those forms of boundary work related to team functioning and performance in ways shaped by the surrounding conditions (Faraj & Yan, 2009).

The study should not be treated as proof that every professional boundary needs simultaneous spanning and reinforcement. Its value here is conceptual: effective knowledge work can involve both movement across boundaries and activities that preserve them.

**Good boundary spanning does not require boundary erasure.**

If every boundary disappears, expertise is flattened into perspective and collaboration can hide the fact that some judgements require years of specialised training. If boundaries become walls, the opposite problem appears: specialists can produce locally correct answers without seeing how those answers interact in the wider system.

The stronger position is movement with preservation. Cross the boundary to access what another field can reveal. Preserve the boundary long enough to remember what gives that field authority. Then integrate without pretending the disciplines have become interchangeable.

Cross the boundary without pretending the boundary disappeared.

### What Did Not Transfer?

We can now return to analogy with a more demanding standard.

When another domain offers a useful pattern, the first question is still important: what transfers? But the second question has equal status: what does not?

Suppose a leader borrows from ecology to think about resilience. The analogy may direct attention to diversity, redundancy, adaptation and interdependence. Useful.

What does not transfer?

People have intentions. Organisations operate under law. Leaders can redesign structures deliberately. Employees can exit, negotiate and resist. Ethical obligations cannot be reduced to survival. The organisation's “environment” is partly created by human institutions rather than simply given by nature.

Those differences do not destroy the analogy. They discipline it.

The same principle applies to every cross-domain move. A military analogy may illuminate coordination under uncertainty while importing assumptions about hierarchy or adversaries that do not fit. A sports analogy may illuminate practice and role clarity while distorting collaboration by making every outcome competitive. A computer analogy may illuminate information processing while hiding embodiment, emotion or social context.

The mature cross-domain thinker becomes interested in the mismatch, not merely tolerant of it, because the mismatch tells us where the imported structure stops carrying explanatory weight.

That is where calibration and analogy meet.

A person who can explain only why the source and target are similar has a connection. A person who can also explain why they are importantly different is beginning to demonstrate judgement.

### The Beginning of Polymathic Judgement

We can now name the capability that has been emerging across the last several chapters.

I call it **polymathic judgement**.

The term is a synthesis for this book, not an established psychological construct or validated scale.

By polymathic judgement, I mean **the capacity to integrate knowledge across domains while calibrating confidence, preserving competence boundaries and bringing deeper expertise into the judgement when one's own understanding reaches its limits**.

Intellectual humility concerns recognising the limits of what we know. Polymathic judgement includes that humility but adds the active work of reasoning across those limits: testing transfer, adjusting claim strength, locating relevant depth and integrating specialist input without pretending that borrowed knowledge has become personal mastery.

That definition is less glamorous than the old picture of the universal genius. Good.

The world does not need more people performing intellectual range. It needs more people capable of using range responsibly.

Polymathic judgement appears when a person can recognise a relevant structure without overclaiming the analogy. It appears when they can say, “I understand enough to see why this field matters, but not enough to make that specialist judgement alone.” It appears when they can listen to conflicting experts without assuming disagreement makes expertise meaningless. It appears when they know where deeper knowledge resides and can translate between the specialist answer and the wider decision.

It also appears in something less dramatic: the willingness to change the strength of a claim.

“I know.”

“I think.”

“The evidence suggests.”

“This analogy may help.”

“I need someone deeper in this field before I go further.”

Those phrases are not merely differences in tone. Used honestly, they are differences in epistemic commitment.

The polymathic thinker should become increasingly precise not only about what they believe, but about the status of the belief. That precision makes breadth safer and more useful because specialists are more likely to trust a boundary-spanner who knows when a claim is tentative and when it is grounded.

The deepest shift is this: polymathy is not the removal of boundaries between disciplines. It is learning which boundaries can be crossed, which differences must be preserved and when another person's depth needs to take over.

The mature polymath is not defined by crossing more boundaries. They are defined partly by knowing what changes when they cross one.

The strongest polymathic thinker is therefore not the person who can answer every question alone. It is the person who can see when the question has crossed a boundary, carry enough understanding across to ask it well, and know whose depth should answer next.

Breadth has now become something more than possession.

It has become judgement.
