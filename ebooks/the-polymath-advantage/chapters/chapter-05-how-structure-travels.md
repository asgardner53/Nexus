# Chapter 5

## How Structure Travels

Clever analogies are cheap.

Organisations are ecosystems. Teams are sports teams. Brains are computers. Markets evolve. AI is a colleague.

Each statement can sound insightful before it has explained anything.

That is the seduction of analogy. A comparison can create an immediate sense of understanding because the unfamiliar suddenly feels familiar. Complexity compresses. The mind recognises a pattern. A difficult problem appears to have acquired shape.

Sometimes that feeling is deserved. Sometimes it is merely fluent.

If an organisation is like an ecosystem, what exactly is being transferred? Interdependence? Adaptation? Competition for scarce resources? Feedback? Selection? Resilience? If none of those relationships is specified, the analogy may be doing little more than decorating the sentence.

The same problem appears whenever knowledge crosses boundaries. A leader hears that ant colonies coordinate without central command and wonders whether the organisation should become more decentralised. An engineer borrows from biology. A strategist borrows from military history. A designer borrows from nature. A technology team describes artificial intelligence as a colleague because the metaphor makes a new system easier to discuss.

The comparison can be useful, but usefulness depends on what is actually crossing the boundary.

**Every analogy is a selective transfer. Its power comes from what it carries. Its danger comes from what it smuggles in.**

This chapter is about the first half of that problem: how a useful structure becomes available in one domain and travels into another.

The previous chapters have already given us some of the ingredients. Breadth increases the number of places from which a useful idea might come. Depth helps us understand what those ideas really mean. Epistemic mobility allows us to change the way a problem is represented.

But none of those guarantees transfer.

You can know something relevant and fail to remember it when it matters. You can retrieve an example and notice the wrong similarity. You can borrow a striking feature while missing the relationship that made the original idea work.

Useful analogy is not simply connection.

**Useful analogy transfers structure, not decoration.**

### Knowledge Has to Come to Mind

One of the strangest things about knowledge is that possession does not guarantee access. We can know something and still fail to use it.

Mary Gick and Keith Holyoak demonstrated this problem in a classic series of experiments on analogical problem solving. Participants were introduced to a story about a military problem in which an attacking force could not safely approach a target along one route in full strength. The solution involved dividing the force, approaching from multiple directions and converging at the target.

Participants later encountered a very different problem involving a medical treatment. The task required finding a way to apply enough radiation to destroy a tumour without damaging the surrounding healthy tissue. The structurally analogous solution was to use multiple weaker rays from different directions so that their effects converged on the tumour.

The domains looked nothing alike. One involved troops and a fortress-like target. The other involved radiation and a tumour. Yet the underlying relationship was similar: a single concentrated path created unacceptable damage, while several weaker paths could converge to produce the required effect at the target.

The important finding was not simply that participants could use the analogy. It was that many failed to do so spontaneously.

People who had encountered the military story were more likely to generate the analogous medical solution when they were explicitly prompted to use the earlier story. Without that hint, transfer was substantially harder (Gick & Holyoak, 1980).

That result is humbling. The relevant knowledge could already be in memory. The person could understand both situations. The connection could be structurally useful. And still it might not come to mind.

**Knowledge cannot transfer if it never comes to mind.**

This gives us a deeper way to understand breadth.

A broad professional may possess many potential source domains: economics, psychology, engineering, history, design, biology, law, operations. That sounds powerful, and it can be. But a large intellectual library is not the same as a good retrieval system.

The right book can be on the shelf and still remain unopened.

Polymathic capability therefore involves more than acquiring varied knowledge. It also involves creating conditions in which structurally relevant knowledge becomes available when the current problem needs it.

Gick and Holyoak's later work offers another clue. When participants compared multiple analogous examples, they could sometimes abstract a more general problem schema, and the quality of that abstraction predicted later transfer. Two examples could help reveal what one example alone left bound to its original story (Gick & Holyoak, 1983).

That moves us from remembering examples to recognising patterns.

### The Best Analogy May Not Look Similar

Why is transfer so difficult?

Part of the answer lies in the difference between what a problem looks like and how it is structured.

Surface features are easy to notice. A hospital and an airport both contain people moving through stages. A company and a sports team both contain groups pursuing goals. A computer network and a transport network both contain nodes and connections.

Those visible similarities can help retrieval because they make one situation remind us of another. But surface similarity does not guarantee useful structure.

Holyoak and Koh examined this tension experimentally and found that both structural and salient surface similarities could influence analogical transfer (Holyoak & Koh, 1987). This matters because the simple story—surface similarity bad, structural similarity good—is too neat.

Surface features can be cognitively useful. They can make a source easier to retrieve. The difficulty is that what makes an analogy easy to remember is not necessarily what makes it intellectually useful.

A source from a distant field may share almost no obvious characteristics with the target problem and still contain exactly the relationship we need.

That produces a beautiful paradox.

**The best analogy may be hiding precisely because it does not look like the problem in front of you.**

The military story and the radiation problem are a good example. Troops are not radiation. Roads are not tissue. A military attack is not medical treatment. If the mind searches only for situations that resemble the target on the surface, the useful source may remain invisible.

The same difficulty appears in professional life.

A leader dealing with overloaded decision-making may search for examples from other organisations because organisations look like organisations. Yet the more useful analogy might come from distributed computing, air-traffic control or biological coordination.

A product team trying to improve customer adoption may look at competitors. The more useful structure might sit in behavioural economics, epidemiology or the diffusion of innovations.

That does not mean distant fields automatically contain better answers. It means surface distance should not disqualify a source before structural similarity has been examined.

### Structure, Not Decoration

Dedre Gentner's structure-mapping theory gives us a more precise way to think about this.

In Gentner's account, analogy involves mapping relations from a base domain onto a target domain. The important correspondences concern how elements relate to one another, with systematic relational structures carrying more weight than isolated shared attributes (Gentner, 1983).

In plain English: the useful part of an analogy is not usually that two things contain similar objects. It is that something important behaves in a similar way.

Consider an illustrative hospital trying to reduce congestion in patient flow.

Someone says, “This is like traffic.”

That could be useful. It could also be useless.

Hospitals contain patients, clinicians, beds, tests, operating theatres and clinical decisions. Roads contain vehicles, lanes, intersections and signals. At the level of objects, the two systems are obviously different.

But some relationships may map. Arrivals can vary unpredictably. Capacity can be constrained. Queues form when demand temporarily exceeds available processing capacity. A blockage downstream can create congestion upstream. Priority rules alter who moves first. Small disruptions can propagate through connected stages.

Now the analogy is doing intellectual work.

It is not saying patients are cars. It is asking whether certain relationships involved in congestion behave similarly enough to help us see the hospital problem differently.

**The useful part of an analogy is rarely that two things look alike. It is that something important behaves alike.**

That distinction also explains why analogies are dangerous when taken literally.

If the traffic analogy encourages the hospital to treat patients as interchangeable units moving through a pipeline, it has imported something that does not belong. Clinical acuity differs. Human needs differ. Ethical obligations differ. Some delays are dangerous; others are appropriate. A queue in a hospital is not merely a queue.

The analogy can still be valuable, but only if we know what we are mapping.

### Expertise Changes Similarity

This is where depth re-enters the story.

A novice and an expert may not merely know different amounts. They may notice different kinds of similarity.

Laura Novick examined analogical transfer using mathematical word problems. Her studies manipulated structural and surface similarity and compared participants with different levels of relevant expertise. In that setting, structurally similar but superficially different problems produced more positive transfer among participants with greater relevant expertise, while misleading surface similarity produced stronger negative transfer among novices (Novick, 1988).

The study is specific. It does not prove that experts in every profession will automatically discover deep analogies across unrelated domains.

What it supports is narrower: relevant expertise can affect sensitivity to structural similarity when surface features differ or mislead.

This reconnects analogy to the argument of Chapter 3.

Depth is not merely the ballast that stops breadth becoming superficial. In some problem-solving settings, it can change the quality of the similarity a person is able to recognise.

**The deeper advantage of expertise may be that similarity itself starts to look different.**

That is my interpretation of the evidence, not Novick's wording, and it should be treated as such.

The point is not that expertise makes people magically analogical. The point is that if useful transfer depends on recognising relationships beneath surface details, then knowing enough to see those relationships matters.

This is why cross-domain borrowing based on shallow familiarity is so risky. Someone may remember a catchy idea from another field without understanding which features are fundamental and which are accidental. The analogy sounds sophisticated because the source is unusual, but novelty of source is not depth of understanding.

### Do Not Merely Collect Examples. Compare Them.

If retrieval and structural recognition matter, can anything be done to strengthen them?

The evidence here becomes particularly important for this book because the central question has always been developmental: can someone become more polymathic?

We should be cautious. No single experiment can establish that a broad capability like polymathy is trainable as a whole. But research on analogical encoding shows that at least one relevant component can be influenced deliberately.

Jeffrey Loewenstein, Leigh Thompson and Dedre Gentner studied knowledge transfer in negotiation. Participants learned from cases that embodied underlying negotiation principles. In one experiment, graduate management students who were asked to draw an analogy between two cases were nearly three times more likely to use the relevant strategy in a later negotiation than participants who had received the same cases separately (Loewenstein, Thompson & Gentner, 1999).

The participants did not receive more cases. They did something different with the cases they had. They compared them.

Later work by Gentner, Loewenstein and Thompson similarly found that comparing examples could help learners abstract common structure and improve later transfer in negotiation learning (Gentner, Loewenstein & Thompson, 2003).

This is a small but meaningful shift in how we think about intellectual breadth.

We often imagine broad learning as accumulation: read more books, attend more courses, learn another field, collect another example. The analogical-encoding research suggests another possibility. What matters is not only how many examples enter memory, but whether the learner compares them deeply enough to recognise what remains constant when the surface changes.

**Do not merely collect examples. Compare them.**

That is not a complete learning system. It is a habit of mind.

Two cases about negotiation may involve different industries, people and commercial stakes. If the reader stores them as two interesting stories, transfer may remain tied to those settings. If the reader asks what structural principle they share, the knowledge has a better chance of becoming mobile.

Comparison pulls attention away from names and circumstances and towards relationships. That is exactly the kind of movement a polymathic thinker needs.

### Remember the Story. Find the Pattern.

This leads to a practical idea I want to offer as synthesis rather than settled cognitive theory.

When you encounter something worth remembering, remember it twice.

First, remember the story. Stories preserve context. They give an idea texture. They remind us of the people, constraints and conditions under which something happened.

Then ask what pattern the story contains.

What relationship mattered? What changed what? What constraint shaped the outcome? What repeated structure might appear somewhere else under different names?

Consider ant colonies.

A person can remember an interesting story about how ants find food. Or they can also extract a more abstract pattern: local signals can sometimes produce coordinated collective behaviour without central command.

The second representation is more portable. It does not make the analogy correct in another domain. It makes the structure available for comparison when a later problem shares the relationship without sharing the setting.

This is where the idea must remain disciplined. I am not claiming the mind literally stores knowledge in two discrete files labelled “story” and “pattern”, nor am I proposing a validated memory architecture. Research on schema abstraction and analogical encoding shows more narrowly that recognising common structure can improve later transfer in specific learning settings (Gick & Holyoak, 1983; Loewenstein, Thompson & Gentner, 1999).

So when we learn broadly, we should not only ask, “What happened?” We should also ask, “What is the relationship here that might travel?”

**Remember the story. Then ask what pattern the story contains.**

This may be one of the differences between possessing broad knowledge and possessing usable breadth.

Two people can encounter the same material. One remembers the topic. The other also remembers the pattern. Representing knowledge at the level of structure may make it easier to recognise relevance when the surface features of a later problem are different. That is an inference from the transfer research, not a complete theory of professional memory.

Imagine a project repeatedly failing because each team optimises its own work while the overall system performs poorly. A person may remember a book about supply chains, a case about hospital flow, a study of traffic congestion and a story about distributed computing. At the level of topic, those examples belong in separate folders. At the level of relationship, they may all contain versions of the same problem: local optimisation interacting with shared constraints to produce poor system-level outcomes.

The project problem no longer has to remind you specifically of a hospital or a computer network. It may instead call up a recurring relationship: parts acting rationally from their own position while degrading the whole.

That is a more demanding way to learn broadly. It is not enough to visit another field and return with vocabulary. The deeper gain comes when the encounter changes the catalogue by which we recognise problems.

A psychologist's concept becomes useful to an engineer not because the engineer can quote psychology, but because the concept becomes relevant when a technical system begins producing a human pattern the engineering vocabulary does not explain well. A principle from ecology becomes useful to a strategist not because ecosystems make attractive metaphors, but because a relationship involving competition, adaptation or interdependence becomes recognisable in a new setting.

This is the point at which breadth starts becoming integration: not when two subjects appear on the same reading list, but when a structure learned in one place becomes available for thought somewhere else.

### Far Does Not Automatically Mean Creative

There is a romantic version of cross-domain thinking in which the most distant analogy produces the biggest creative breakthrough.

An engineer studies a leaf and invents a revolutionary material. A strategist studies an ant colony and redesigns the company. A surgeon watches Formula One and transforms hospital handovers.

Sometimes dramatic cross-domain inspiration does occur. But the general story is too tidy.

Joel Chan and Christian Schunn examined actual brainstorming conversations in a professional engineering design team. Their study focused on far analogies: analogies drawn from conceptually distant sources. Far analogy use was associated with a higher rate of concept generation, which sounds like the familiar creativity story (Chan & Schunn, 2015).

Then the result became more interesting.

The concepts that followed far analogies were not simply huge jumps away from the concepts that came immediately before them. In fact, they were more similar to preceding concepts than in baseline conditions. The authors argued that far analogies may contribute through other pathways, including more iterative and deeper exploration within the problem space (Chan & Schunn, 2015).

That finding is useful because it punctures the mythology without killing the mechanism.

A far analogy may matter. It just may not matter in the way the heroic innovation story suggests.

**A distant analogy does not necessarily catapult thought somewhere new. Sometimes it gives thought a different way to dig where it already is.**

That is a more believable account of professional creativity. New ideas often emerge through revision, combination, testing and deeper exploration rather than one cinematic leap from an unrelated domain.

The analogy changes the search. The work still has to be done.

### The Analogy Has to Earn Its Keep

At this point, analogy begins to look powerful. It can help retrieve knowledge, reveal deeper structure, support abstraction and stimulate exploration.

That is precisely when we need to become suspicious.

A good analogy creates a dangerous psychological reward: familiarity. The target problem starts to feel understandable because we now have something else through which to see it. That feeling can be useful. It can also arrive before the analogy has been tested.

An organisation described as an ecosystem may suddenly appear to have ecological properties it does not possess. A brain described as a computer may encourage people to imagine memory as though it were digital storage. A team described as a family may import expectations of loyalty that become unhealthy in an employment relationship. AI described as a colleague may encourage trust, accountability or social assumptions the system cannot actually bear.

The analogy has made the unfamiliar familiar. Now it has to earn its keep.

A useful analogy should do something more than sound elegant. It should improve the representation of the problem, reveal a relationship we had missed, generate a worthwhile hypothesis or suggest a new question that can be tested.

And it should remain provisional.

**Analogy is a bridge for thought, not a certificate of truth.**

That distinction is fundamental to polymathic capability.

The goal is not to become the person in the meeting with the most surprising comparisons. The goal is to become better at recognising when another domain contains a relationship worth examining, retrieving that relationship, mapping it carefully and remaining sufficiently sceptical to ask where the correspondence ends.

That last step is the one we have not yet fully addressed.

Because every analogy has a boundary.

The military story helped participants see the radiation problem differently, but soldiers and radiation are not the same thing. Traffic can illuminate congestion in a hospital, but patients are not vehicles. Ecosystems can teach us something about interdependence, but organisations are not biological systems governed by natural selection in the same way.

A good analogy can make an unfamiliar problem suddenly intelligible.

That feeling is useful. It is also dangerous.

The moment the target begins to feel familiar is exactly when we need to ask what did not transfer.

Because every bridge has an end.
