# Chapter 3

## Breadth Is Not the Advantage

Breadth has excellent public relations.

It sounds curious, adaptable, creative and modern. It suggests an open mind, a willingness to learn and a refusal to become trapped inside one professional box. In a world worried about silos, narrow expertise and rapidly changing work, breadth seems almost automatically virtuous.

Depth has a less glamorous image. It can sound narrow, specialist, technical, perhaps even slightly old-fashioned. It evokes the person who knows everything about one tiny corner of the world and little beyond it.

That contrast is tempting. It is also misleading.

A surgeon with broad interests but shallow clinical knowledge is not an improvement on a specialist surgeon. A cybersecurity professional who knows a little about behavioural science, economics, design, artificial intelligence and geopolitics is not more useful if they cannot recognise a serious vulnerability in the system in front of them. A financial adviser does not become wiser merely by adding philosophy podcasts to the commute.

Breadth can enlarge a mind. It can also decorate one.

**Range without substance is not polymathy. It is tourism.**

This is where the popular image of the polymath can lead us astray. Once we reject the idea that polymathy means becoming another Leonardo, it is easy to swing too far in the other direction. We start celebrating range for its own sake. Read more widely. Learn more disciplines. Build more interests. Become a generalist.

All of that can be useful. None of it is sufficient.

The argument of this chapter is simple, but it has consequences for the rest of the book:

**Breadth creates possibilities. Depth gives them substance. Integration converts the relationship into value.**

Breadth and depth are not enemies. They make different things possible.

### Breadth Has Excellent Public Relations

The appeal of breadth is understandable.

Breadth gives us options. It exposes us to unfamiliar ideas. It introduces different ways of framing a problem. It can make conversation richer and professional judgement less parochial. Someone who has encountered several disciplines has more places to search when the familiar answer stops working.

Breadth also carries a kind of cultural prestige. The widely read executive appears more interesting than the one who speaks only the language of quarterly performance. The engineer who understands psychology seems more rounded than the engineer who discusses only systems. The scientist who can move between biology, computation and statistics looks better equipped for an interdisciplinary age.

Sometimes those impressions are justified. Sometimes they are merely impressions.

The danger is confusing exposure with capability.

Knowing that a field exists is not the same as knowing how that field reasons. Recognising its vocabulary is not the same as understanding the distinctions that matter inside it. Being able to repeat a model is not the same as knowing when the model applies, what assumptions it contains or where it breaks.

Breadth increases the territory available to us. Depth changes the resolution at which we can see it.

A novice and an expert can look at the same situation and organise it differently. In a classic series of studies, Michelene Chi, Paul Feltovich and Robert Glaser compared how expert and novice physicists represented physics problems. Novices tended to group problems according to visible surface features. Experts were more likely to organise them around the deeper physical principles needed for solution (Chi, Feltovich & Glaser, 1981).

The study was about physics, not universal human expertise. But it demonstrates something important about depth.

Expertise is not simply a larger pile of facts. It can change the structure of what a person sees.

The expert notices relationships the novice has not yet learned to recognise. What appears to be one kind of problem at the surface may belong to another category underneath. A familiar-looking solution may contain a hidden assumption. A small variation may matter enormously because the expert understands what the novice does not yet know to inspect.

**Depth is not merely knowing more. It is seeing finer differences.**

That matters for polymathic thinking because useful connection depends on discrimination. If you borrow an idea from another field without enough understanding to distinguish its central principle from its superficial features, the connection may sound clever while adding very little. If you know enough about a field to recognise what makes an idea work, you have a better chance of carrying something useful across the boundary.

Breadth gives us more things to connect. Depth gives those connections something to grip.

### What Depth Gives You

Most experienced professionals know this intuitively because they have lived through the difference between familiarity and competence.

Early in a career, a field often appears simpler than it later becomes. You learn the terminology and standard methods. You become confident enough to produce work. Then experience reveals the complications. Exceptions appear. Rules acquire conditions. Context starts to matter. Two situations that once looked identical stop looking identical.

In many professions, becoming genuinely good means becoming less impressed by simple answers. With experience, people often begin to recognise distinctions and recurring structures that were invisible when they first entered the field.

This does not make experts infallible. Expertise can become stale. Experts can overgeneralise from familiar environments. They can become attached to established methods and fail to notice when the problem itself has changed.

Depth is not a guarantee of wisdom, but neither is breadth. The interesting question is not which one should win. It is what each contributes.

That question becomes clearer when we look at people whose work was explicitly measured across both dimensions.

### The 3M Inventors

Researchers Wai Fong Boh, Roberto Evaristo and Andrew Ouderkirk examined inventors at 3M, a company known for research and product innovation. Their study separated two dimensions that are often collapsed into one: the breadth of an inventor's expertise and its depth (Boh, Evaristo & Ouderkirk, 2014).

Breadth and depth were not treated as opposite ends of a single line. A person could have more of one without necessarily having less of the other.

The researchers then looked at different outcomes. Breadth of expertise was associated with generating a larger number of inventions. Depth of expertise was associated with technically influential inventions. Inventors who combined both breadth and depth were especially valuable in the study's measure of career success: converting inventions into commercially successful products within 3M (Boh, Evaristo & Ouderkirk, 2014).

The finding is easy to turn into a slogan. We should resist that.

This was one organisation, in a research-intensive industrial setting, using specific measures of invention, technical influence and commercial success. It does not establish a universal law that every professional should maximise both breadth and depth, nor that the same balance produces the same outcomes in medicine, law, leadership or education.

What it does give us is a useful empirical correction to the specialist-versus-generalist debate.

Different expertise profiles can contribute differently. A broad inventor may have access to more potential combinations and avenues of search. A deeply specialised inventor may produce work with greater technical influence inside a domain. Someone who combines substantial depth with broader knowledge may be able to create and commercialise in ways that draw on both.

**The point is not that the polymath beats the specialist.**

The study is more interesting than that. It suggests there are several ways expertise can create value, and that breadth and depth are not mutually exclusive forms of intellectual capital.

That matters beyond 3M. A deeply specialised engineer may solve a problem nobody else in the organisation can solve. A boundary-spanning project lead may see dependencies that specialists working separately cannot see. A product manager may not be the deepest expert in any one discipline but may be effective at integrating customer, technical and commercial constraints.

The mistake is assuming one profile should replace the others.

Polymathic capability is not a campaign against specialists. It is a way of understanding how people can extend the usefulness of depth without pretending that every role demands the same range.

Nor is the point to prescribe a particular shape of expertise. Some people develop depth early and broaden later. Others explore widely before finding one or two areas they want to deepen. Careers can alternate between periods of range and concentration. Useful polymathic capability requires meaningful depth somewhere; it does not require that depth always came first.

That is why “be broader” is weak advice. Breadth has to be useful for something.

The same distinction matters when people describe themselves as generalists. Generalism can be a valuable professional orientation, especially in roles that require coordination across functions. But breadth becomes more consequential when it rests on enough experience somewhere to distinguish signal from noise. The person who has wrestled seriously with one demanding field knows, at least in one domain, what it feels like for apparent simplicity to dissolve under scrutiny. That experience can create a useful caution when entering another field: the first explanation is probably not the whole explanation.

This does not mean people without a long-established specialism are disqualified from polymathic development. Someone early in a career may still be exploring widely and only later discover where they want to go deep. Another person may build two areas in parallel. A third may develop depth through practice rather than formal credentials. The requirement is not a particular career sequence. It is eventually having enough substantive engagement somewhere that cross-domain thinking is disciplined by more than enthusiasm.

That point also changes how we interpret breadth professionally. A broad colleague is not valuable because they can contribute an opinion to every topic. Their value appears when they can recognise relationships that matter, know enough to ask questions that specialists respect, and avoid pretending that familiarity equals authority. Breadth can make collaboration easier because it gives people more shared language. But the moment shared language is mistaken for interchangeable expertise, breadth begins to undermine the very depth it was meant to connect.

### The Cost of Moving Too Far

If the 3M study stops us treating breadth and depth as enemies, more recent research stops us romanticising intellectual distance.

In 2025, Ryan Hill and colleagues published a large study in *Nature* examining what happens when scientists and inventors move away from their previous areas of work. The researchers developed a measure of how far a new paper or patent departed from a researcher's existing portfolio and applied it across millions of scientific publications and patents (Hill et al., 2025).

They found what they called a **pivot penalty**.

On average, the further researchers moved from their previous work, the lower the impact of the new work. Larger pivots were also associated with lower publication success, less market impact in patents and weaker engagement with established combinations of prior knowledge (Hill et al., 2025).

A research pivot is not the same thing as learning another discipline, and these findings should not be treated as a general penalty for curiosity. The study concerns scientific research and patenting, where performance is measured in specific ways.

Even within that setting, the result is not an argument for staying in your lane. The same paper notes that remaining within an existing direction can produce diminishing returns and cause researchers to miss opportunities elsewhere. Exploration remains potentially valuable. The problem is that exploration carries costs (Hill et al., 2025).

That tension matters for polymathic thinking.

Moving into unfamiliar territory may expose you to ideas your field cannot easily produce from within itself. But distance also strips away some of the advantages your expertise gives you. You know less of the literature. You recognise fewer hidden assumptions. You have weaker intuition about what is routine and what is genuinely novel. You may not even know which questions reveal competence.

Novelty and ignorance can feel surprisingly similar from inside an unfamiliar field.

This is one reason outsiders sometimes see what insiders miss and sometimes merely rediscover what insiders solved years ago. The further we travel from established expertise, the less casually we can trust our own confidence.

That does not make distant exploration a mistake. It makes it expensive. There is more to learn, more to verify and more reason to seek people whose depth exceeds our own.

The polymathic advantage therefore cannot come from maximising intellectual distance. It comes from learning how to move beyond an anchor without pretending the anchor is irrelevant.

There is a useful humility in that conclusion. Crossing a boundary should increase curiosity and reduce certainty at the same time. The unfamiliar field may contain exactly the idea we need, but we should expect to misunderstand some of it on first contact. That is not a reason to stay home. It is a reason to travel with better questions, stronger sources and a willingness to be corrected by people who have spent longer there.

### An Anchor, Not an Anchor Chain

An intellectual anchor is the area, or small number of areas, where a person has developed enough depth to recognise quality, understand constraints and generate informed questions. It is where they possess something stronger than familiarity.

For many readers, that anchor will be the expertise that built their career. The polymathic move is not to discard that depth in pursuit of a broader identity. It is to use depth as a point of departure.

**Depth is not the prison polymathy escapes. It is the platform from which meaningful range becomes possible.**

Consider a cybersecurity architect who becomes interested in human behaviour.

The obvious response is to say that cybersecurity is partly a people problem and therefore the architect should learn psychology. True enough. But the value does not come from adding “psychology” to a list of interests.

It comes when behavioural knowledge changes the way the architect thinks about security controls.

Why do people bypass a process designed to protect them? What happens when secure behaviour is much harder than insecure behaviour? How do repeated warnings change attention? When does a control shift risk rather than reduce it?

The architect's technical depth still matters. It allows the behavioural questions to be asked inside a real security environment rather than as generic observations about human nature. And behavioural knowledge does not replace specialist psychology. It helps the architect recognise when another lens matters, ask better questions and know when deeper expertise should be brought into the work.

This is the relationship we are after: not escape from expertise, but expansion from expertise.

The point is not to prescribe a T-shaped, comb-shaped or any other fashionable geometry of talent. The point is simpler. Breadth becomes more useful when it connects to knowledge substantial enough to interrogate what has been found.

### Near Enough to Recognise, Far Enough to Surprise

Not all breadth is equally distant from what we already know.

Some exploration sits close enough to existing expertise that much of the conceptual terrain remains familiar. A software engineer learning systems architecture is broadening, but the bridge is visible. A financial analyst learning economics may encounter different models and methods while retaining a familiar language of incentives, markets and evidence.

Other exploration is more distant. An engineer studies anthropology. A lawyer studies network theory. A physician studies design. A strategist studies ecology. The conceptual distance may create more surprising possibilities because the imported field carries different assumptions, methods and metaphors.

For convenience, we can think of some exploration as relatively adjacent to our existing expertise and some as more distant. This is descriptive language, not a formal taxonomy.

The distinction matters because distance changes both opportunity and risk.

The closer a field is to our expertise, the easier it may be to evaluate what transfers, but the less radically different its perspective may be. The farther away it is, the more surprising the connection may become, but the more difficult it is to judge whether the connection is real.

The practical implication is not to create a map with neat rings around your profession. Knowledge does not arrange itself so politely. What feels adjacent to one person may be distant to another because prior education and experience create different bridges.

The useful question is personal and problem-specific: **how much new intellectual infrastructure do I need before I can use this field responsibly?**

That question turns breadth from a personality trait into a learning decision. It asks not simply whether a new domain is interesting, but how far away it is from what you already understand, what you would need to learn before borrowing from it, and whose expertise you should involve while you are still building your own.

We will deal with that problem directly when we examine analogy and transfer. For now, it is enough to recognise that there is a difference between expanding around an existing domain and leaping into an intellectual world with unfamiliar standards of evidence, language and practice.

Both can be valuable. Neither should be romanticised.

### Breadth Is a Search Space

This gives us a more useful way to think about breadth.

Breadth is not a trophy cabinet. It is a search space.

The broader your meaningful exposure, the more places you can look when the problem in front of you does not yield to the usual approach. A designer may borrow from biology. A leader may borrow from systems theory. A scientist may encounter a method developed in another discipline. An engineer may recognise that a technical failure is partly a behavioural problem.

Breadth increases the number of possible starting points.

That is valuable, but search has a cost.

Every hour spent exploring one domain is an hour not spent deepening another. Every new field brings terminology, foundational ideas, debates and methods that take time to understand. Breadth therefore carries an opportunity cost even when learning is enjoyable.

This is where indiscriminate curiosity can become a problem. A person can spend years moving from interesting idea to interesting idea and mistake movement for development. The mind feels busy. The reading list grows. Connections appear everywhere. Yet nothing becomes deep enough to test, build or use.

Serious polymathic development requires selection.

**Not every curiosity deserves a curriculum.**

Some interests can remain interests. Some fields deserve enough familiarity to improve conversation. Some deserve working competence. A very small number may justify the effort required for genuine depth.

The skill is not only opening intellectual doors. It is deciding which ones to walk through and how far.

This is why breadth should be judged by relevance and generative value rather than by volume. Ten loosely connected subjects may contribute less than one carefully chosen neighbouring discipline that changes the way you work. Conversely, one distant field may be worth years of attention if it repeatedly reveals structures your home discipline cannot easily see.

The count of subjects tells us very little. The quality of the relationship among them tells us much more.

A larger search space can also produce more bad candidates. If ten fields offer ten models, you now have ten ways to be wrong.

Depth helps with selection. It gives us some basis for asking whether the imported idea fits the problem, whether it respects the constraints and whether the analogy preserves what actually matters.

Integration comes after that. It is what happens when the imported knowledge does not merely sit beside existing expertise but changes the way the problem is understood or acted upon.

This is why breadth itself is not the advantage.

Breadth is the possibility of advantage.

**Breadth increases where you can look. Depth helps you recognise what is worth bringing home.**

That distinction protects the book from two opposite forms of intellectual vanity.

The first is the vanity of narrow expertise: the belief that because you know one field deeply, every important problem can eventually be forced into its language.

The second is the vanity of superficial range: the belief that because you have encountered many ideas, you understand enough to combine them freely.

Polymathic capability requires something harder than either. It asks us to respect what depth provides while becoming mobile enough to search beyond it.

That balance will look different for different people and different problems. A surgeon should not broaden at the expense of surgical competence. A research scientist may need to go deeper before a distant collaboration becomes productive. A senior leader may require less technical depth in every individual function but more ability to recognise which forms of specialist knowledge a decision depends on.

The architecture changes with the work. The principle does not.

**The advantage does not lie in breadth itself. It lies in what depth and breadth can do together.**

And even that leaves one problem unresolved.

A person can possess considerable depth and considerable breadth and still use the same mental frame every time. They can know economics, psychology and technology yet approach every organisational problem as though it were primarily financial. They can possess several lenses and habitually reach for only one.

Knowledge does not move merely because it exists in the same mind.

A broad mind can contain many lenses and still look through the same one every time.

The real shift begins when a person can recognise that the problem in front of them may need to be seen differently.

That is not a question of how much you know.

It is a question of whether you can change lenses.
