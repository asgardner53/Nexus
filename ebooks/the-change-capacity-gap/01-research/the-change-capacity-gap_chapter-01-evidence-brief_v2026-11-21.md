## 1. Chapter purpose

Chapter 1 must make the reader see the problem differently.

The organisation tends to govern change through discrete programs, projects and initiatives. Employees, managers and operational systems do not experience those interventions discretely. They experience the combined demands that arrive through the same calendars, workflows, teams, meetings, systems, decisions and attention.

The chapter's job is not to prove that every organisation is overloaded. It is to establish the governing mismatch that makes the Change Capacity Gap plausible and worth investigating.

## 2. Governing argument

> **Leaders manage change as initiatives. People experience change as cumulative load.**

The opening chapter should establish three propositions:

1. organisations commonly run multiple changes at the same time;
2. those changes often draw on overlapping pools of people, attention, budget, managerial capacity and sensemaking bandwidth; and
3. initiative-by-initiative governance can therefore miss the total change demand experienced by the organisation.

This is the conceptual doorway into the rest of the book.

## 3. Reader belief on entry

A senior reader may believe:

- each transformation initiative has its own sponsor, governance, resources and change plan;
- workload and fatigue are local implementation issues;
- if every program is individually justified, the total portfolio is probably manageable; and
- resistance can be addressed through better communication, training or engagement.

The chapter should destabilise these assumptions without caricaturing them.

## 4. Reader belief on exit

By the end of the chapter, the reader should be considering a different question:

> **What if the problem is not any single change initiative, but the total load created by all of them together?**

The reader should understand that the unit of governance and the unit of human experience may be different.

## 5. Evidence propositions

### C01-001 — Organisations frequently implement multiple major changes simultaneously

**Evidence target:** Establish prevalence, not a universal threshold.

**Candidate evidence:**

- UN System Staff College's *State of Change Management in the UN System* reports that, in 2025, organisations in its sample were implementing an average of six organisational change initiatives simultaneously.
- The same research reports that 68% of respondents described their organisations as past, at or nearing change saturation, and 49% identified saturation as a principal challenge.

**Use:** Contemporary illustrative evidence of concurrent organisational change.

**Qualification:** This is evidence from the UN system, not a global cross-industry benchmark. Do not generalise the exact six-initiative figure to all organisations.

### C01-002 — Multiple changes can compete for shared resources and sensemaking bandwidth

**Evidence target:** Support the core cumulative-load mechanism.

**Candidate evidence:**

- Reske (2026) explicitly argues that simultaneous organisational changes can compete for the same people, attention, budget and employee sensemaking bandwidth.
- Stensaker, Rydland and Colman (2025) study managers orchestrating parallel and overlapping organisational changes and securing sufficient capacity to advance them.

**Use:** Strong conceptual warrant that changes interact through shared organisational resources rather than remaining isolated.

**Qualification:** Both Academy of Management sources are conference-proceedings or abstract-level evidence. They are highly relevant to the architecture but require full-text or final-publication verification before carrying consequential manuscript claims.

### C01-003 — Repeated change is associated with reported workload and wellbeing strain

**Evidence target:** Establish that cumulative change has observable human consequences without claiming simple causality.

**Candidate evidence:**

- Deloitte's 2026 Global Human Capital Trends material reports that one-third of surveyed workers experienced more than 15 major changes in the preceding year; respondents also reported decreased wellbeing (68%) and increased workload (60%) associated with sustained change.
- Only 27% of surveyed leaders reported that their organisations manage change effectively.

**Use:** Contemporary global workforce context and an opening signal that the issue is not marginal.

**Qualification:** Deloitte research is organisation-authored survey research and should be treated as interested evidence. The figures describe survey responses and associations, not proof that change alone caused each outcome.

### C01-004 — Overlapping initiatives are associated with higher reported change fatigue

**Evidence target:** Strengthen the link between concurrency and cumulative experience.

**Candidate evidence:**

- Deloitte's October 2026 change-management research reports that respondents at organisations typically running four or more large-scale initiatives concurrently were 6.4 times more likely to report high change fatigue than organisations running only one.
- The same report states that 27% of respondents said their organisations had no formal process for tracking or managing total change load, and 54% said no one had authority to slow or pause an initiative based on employee change capacity.

**Use:** Directly relevant current evidence for the book's governance mismatch.

**Qualification:** The source is recent vendor/professional-services research based on executive survey responses. Methodology must be opened and checked before final claim matching. Do not present odds/relative likelihood as causation.

### C01-005 — The Change Capacity Gap is an integrative author construct, not an established scientific scale

**Evidence target:** Protect originality and evidentiary honesty.

**Author synthesis:**

The book's concept integrates existing evidence on organisational change capacity, overlapping changes, change fatigue, resource competition, sensemaking and portfolio governance.

**Required wording control:** The Change Capacity Gap may be introduced as Alec Gardner's integrative construct for the mismatch between total change demand and available change absorption capacity. It must not be represented as a validated universal measure unless later empirical work supports that claim.

## 6. Three-layer evidence test

For each consequential passage, preserve:

1. **Evidence establishes:** what the research actually reports.
2. **Reasonable interpretation:** what can fairly be inferred from that evidence.
3. **Gardner synthesis:** how the book integrates those findings into the Change Capacity Gap argument.

### Example

**Evidence establishes:** Concurrent changes can draw on the same resources and employees' sensemaking bandwidth.

**Reasonable interpretation:** Initiative-by-initiative planning can underestimate interactions between simultaneous changes.

**Gardner synthesis:** The organisation may govern separate initiatives while people experience one combined change load.

These layers must not be collapsed into a single unsupported causal claim.

## 7. Counterarguments and disconfirming evidence

Chapter 1 should not imply that concurrency is inherently dysfunctional.

Evidence from Stensaker, Rydland and Colman indicates that managers can actively orchestrate multiple overlapping changes and secure sufficient capacity and support. This is important because it prevents the book from becoming an argument for simply doing less change.

The stronger proposition is:

> **Multiple change is not the problem by itself. Unseen, ungoverned or poorly orchestrated cumulative load is the problem.**

The manuscript should also recognise that:

- urgent external conditions can make simultaneous change unavoidable;
- organisations differ substantially in capability, slack, trust, leadership and prior change history;
- some employees may experience simultaneous changes differently from others; and
- successful orchestration can increase capacity rather than merely consume it.

## 8. Chapter boundaries

Chapter 1 should **not** fully develop:

- change fatigue — Chapter 2;
- accumulation, collision and translation load — Chapter 3;
- middle-manager dynamics — Chapter 4;
- the full multidimensional definition of change absorption capacity — Chapter 5;
- stabilisation — Chapter 6;
- governance remedies — Chapters 8–10.

Chapter 1 names the hidden mismatch and makes it intellectually credible. It should leave the mechanism partially unresolved.

## 9. Potential opening

A useful opening should place the reader in a recognisable executive setting: a portfolio review in which each program appears rational and adequately governed, followed by the view from a manager or team receiving all of those demands at once.

This can be written as a composite organisational scene only if clearly presented as illustrative rather than factual. A verified real-world case would be preferable if one emerges in research.

## 10. Candidate chapter structure

### Opening — The portfolio meeting

Show the contrast between a clean executive portfolio view and the messy cumulative experience lower in the organisation.

### The invisible total

Introduce concurrent change and the problem of counting projects instead of experienced load.

### What the organisation cannot see from the portfolio

Use UNSSC and overlapping-change research to show shared resources, attention and sensemaking.

### When reasonable initiatives become unreasonable together

Introduce the idea that local rationality can produce system-level strain.

### The first definition of the Change Capacity Gap

Introduce the concept carefully as an integrative author construct, not a validated scale.

### End point

Leave the reader with the question of whether fatigue and resistance are causes, failures or signals — opening Chapter 2.
