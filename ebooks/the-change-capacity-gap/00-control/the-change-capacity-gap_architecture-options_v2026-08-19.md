## Architecture test criteria

Each option was tested against five criteria:

1. Does every chapter advance the central argument rather than repeat generic change-management advice?
2. Does the sequence move from diagnosis to explanation to leadership action?
3. Can the evidence base support the chapter without overstating certainty?
4. Does the architecture create a distinctive Nexus BMG thought-leadership position?
5. Can the book generate useful derivative products, workshops and diagnostics without reading like a consulting brochure?

# Option A — The System Failure Architecture

## Core logic

Begin with the visible symptom of change fatigue, reveal the system failure underneath it, then build a leadership model for governing cumulative change load.

This is the strongest thought-leadership structure because it takes the reader through a conceptual reversal: fatigue is not primarily a people problem; it is often a signal that the organisation has exceeded its capacity to absorb change.

## Proposed chapters

### 1. The Organisation Sees Projects. People Feel Load.

Open with the central tension. Leaders review portfolios, programs and initiatives separately. Employees experience all of them at once. Establish the Change Capacity Gap and distinguish it from ordinary workload, resistance to change and burnout.

**Chapter job:** Name the hidden problem.

### 2. Change Fatigue Is a Signal, Not a Diagnosis

Examine what leaders often misread as resistance, disengagement or low resilience. Show how repeated change can generate withdrawal, cynicism, reduced discretionary effort and weaker sensemaking without implying that every negative reaction is caused by change overload.

**Chapter job:** Reframe the symptom.

### 3. How the Gap Opens

Develop the first three mechanisms: accumulation, collision and translation load. Explain why individually sensible initiatives become collectively unmanageable when they draw on the same people, managers, decision forums and operational systems.

**Chapter job:** Explain the mechanism.

### 4. The Middle Manager Becomes the Shock Absorber

Explore the position of managers who must translate strategy, maintain performance, answer employee questions, implement new tools, resolve competing priorities and absorb emotional friction. Avoid the tired “frozen middle” trope. Treat middle management as a structural translation layer whose capacity is often taken for granted.

**Chapter job:** Humanise where the system strain concentrates.

### 5. Every Change Consumes More Than Time

Show that change consumes cognitive bandwidth, attention, trust, learning capacity, decision quality and social coordination, not merely project hours. Introduce the idea of absorption capacity as multi-dimensional.

**Chapter job:** Broaden the definition of capacity.

### 6. Recovery Is Part of Change

Develop the recovery-deficit mechanism. Organisations often move from implementation to the next initiative without allowing behaviours, systems and relationships to stabilise. Argue that stability is not the opposite of change; it is part of successful change.

**Chapter job:** Establish stabilisation as a leadership responsibility.

### 7. The Performance Feedback Trap

Explain how overloaded change systems can create slower execution, missed benefits, weak adoption and declining confidence. Leaders may interpret those outcomes as evidence that more intervention is needed, producing yet more change and widening the gap.

**Chapter job:** Show why the problem can become self-reinforcing.

### 8. Stop Governing Change One Initiative at a Time

Shift from diagnosis to governance. Argue for portfolio-level visibility of total change load, dependencies, timing, affected populations, critical roles, managerial translation demands and competing capability requirements.

**Chapter job:** Reframe the unit of governance.

### 9. The Leadership Decisions That Restore Capacity

Develop the practical levers: sequence, stop, remove, resource, simplify, clarify, protect, stabilise and learn. Emphasise that “not now” and “stop” are legitimate transformation decisions.

**Chapter job:** Turn the concept into executive choices.

### 10. Building an Organisation That Can Absorb Change

Explore the longer-term capacity system: leadership quality, trust, psychological safety, role clarity, decision rights, organisational slack, learning capability, workforce capability and credible change governance.

**Chapter job:** Move from load management to capacity building.

### 11. AI Will Widen the Gap Unless Leaders Change the System

Use AI as the contemporary stress test. AI can accelerate the rate at which organisations can initiate redesign, automation and experimentation, while human adaptation, workflow redesign, judgement and governance remain constrained. Connect directly to Nexus BMG's human-centred AI positioning.

**Chapter job:** Make the argument urgent and forward-looking.

### 12. The Change Capacity Organisation

Close with the leadership model. A mature organisation does not simply become “good at change”. It becomes disciplined about total load, sequencing, recovery, human capacity and stopping decisions. Present the final integrated Change Capacity model and executive questions.

**Chapter job:** Synthesis and memorable destination.

## Strengths

- Strong conceptual reversal and narrative momentum.
- Clear distinction from generic change-management books.
- Gives the five provisional mechanisms natural homes.
- Creates a strong executive and commercial position.
- AI chapter adds contemporary urgency without making the book an AI book.
- Supports a future diagnostic, workshop and executive briefing naturally.

## Risks

- Requires disciplined evidence to avoid making “change overload” explain every organisational problem.
- Chapters 5 and 6 could overlap unless capacity and recovery are carefully distinguished.
- Chapter 10 must avoid becoming a generic list of healthy-organisation practices.

# Option B — The Human Experience Architecture

## Core logic

Start inside the employee and manager experience, then widen outward to organisational design and governance.

This option would be more emotionally resonant and accessible, but it risks positioning the book too close to wellbeing, burnout and employee-experience literature.

## Proposed chapters

1. Why People Stop Believing the Next Change Will Be Different
2. The Accumulation Effect
3. Attention Is Finite
4. Trust Has a Change Cost
5. The Manager in the Middle
6. Learning While Running
7. When Adaptation Becomes Withdrawal
8. The Organisation Behind the Fatigue
9. Rebuilding Space to Change
10. Leading Without Adding More Noise
11. Change Capacity in the AI Era
12. Sustainable Adaptation

## Strengths

- Strong human narrative.
- High reader recognition and emotional relevance.
- Natural fit for HR, People and Culture and leadership audiences.
- Easier to illustrate through stories and workplace scenarios.

## Risks

- Can drift toward employee wellbeing or resilience.
- Organisational governance arrives relatively late.
- Less differentiated commercially from existing change-fatigue commentary.
- Primary executive audience may perceive it as a “people issue” book rather than an organisational-performance book.

# Option C — The Executive Governance Architecture

## Core logic

Treat change capacity as an enterprise governance problem from the outset, organised around the decisions senior leaders must make.

This is the most commercial and executive-facing option, but also the one most at risk of feeling like a consulting methodology rather than a thought-leadership book.

## Proposed chapters

1. Your Change Portfolio Is Not Your Change Load
2. The Hidden Capacity Constraint
3. Who Owns Total Change Load?
4. Mapping Organisational Exposure
5. Where Change Collides
6. The Managerial Capacity Bottleneck
7. Sequencing and Dependency
8. What to Stop
9. Designing Recovery and Stabilisation
10. Building Change Capacity
11. Governing AI-Driven Transformation
12. The Change Capacity Operating Model

## Strengths

- Very clear executive relevance.
- Strong platform for diagnostics and advisory services.
- Makes governance accountability explicit.
- Easy to turn into workshops and portfolio-review tools.

## Risks

- Can feel procedural.
- Less narrative and human texture.
- More likely to date as management methods evolve.
- Risks becoming a “how-to transformation portfolio” book rather than a durable idea-led work.

## Recommendation

**Recommend Option A — The System Failure Architecture.**

It best protects the book's distinctive intellectual territory. The reader first recognises the familiar phenomenon of change fatigue, then discovers that the more important problem is structural: the organisation has been governing change in units that do not match how people experience it.

The central movement becomes:

> **Fatigue → cumulative load → system mechanisms → governance failure → leadership choices → organisational capacity.**

That gives the book both human relevance and executive consequence.

## Architecture conditions before approval

Before this architecture should be locked, research must test four potential weaknesses:

1. whether “absorption capacity” can be defined clearly enough to support Chapters 5 and 10 without becoming vague;
2. whether the performance feedback trap is sufficiently evidenced or should remain an original interpretive model rather than a causal claim;
3. whether enough strong evidence exists to distinguish stabilisation/recovery from ordinary implementation and learning; and
4. whether the AI chapter materially advances the book rather than simply adding topicality.
